# A system keeps its own records beside the user's data, writes the record of a read later, and reclaims on a schedule someone else sets; the kernel store agrees with that and departs in eleven places

The question was the one Article 3 of `context/standards/constitution.md` puts to every spec: where Unix, Linux, FreeBSD and Plan 9 have a convention for what this spec decides, does the spec follow it, and where it does not, is the departure stated with its reason. Thirteen local clones were read: four operating systems, two database engines and SQLAlchemy, and six frameworks that keep state in an application's database. The reading was done in one sitting of about two hours, in three parts read side by side, and 34 of the citations gathered were opened a second time and found as cited; those used below are marked. ## The answer

The spec's large choices are the conventional ones. A file system keeps its own bookkeeping inside the store it serves, under names the user's names do not reach, and an external place for it is an option and never the default: ext4's journal is inode 8, FFS's is a file in the root directory, SQLite's and Postgres's catalogs are tables in the same file and the same database. The record of a read is made after the read, in memory, by a call that cannot fail the read, and a crash loses it: that is `touch_atime`, FFS's `IN_ACCESS` and Postgres's statistics. Expired state is removed by a job somebody schedules, and the cleaner of `/tmp` ships turned off: that is FreeBSD's `periodic`, Plan 9's absent cleaner, fossil's snapshots at their default, and SQLite's `VACUUM`. So decisions 4, 5, 13, 14 and 17 follow Unix and Plan 9 as far as a convention exists.

The spec departs in eleven places, listed in section 8. Seven of them it already states with a reason, though none as a departure from Unix or Plan 9 and none with the expiry Article 3 asks for. Four it does not: that a record has no path is reasoned from Article 1 and never set against Plan 9, which would give it one; that a lost stamp refuses a later change is stated as a consequence, where a lost access time refuses nothing; that no public call waits for delayed records, where Unix has `fsync`, is stated as ruled with no reason; and that the names it reserves are reserved by a lint and by neither engine is not stated at all. The spec has no numbered decision for Article 3 and no section on prior art; the text for both closes this memo.

## 1. A read leaves its record after it returns, in memory, and never fails for it

- **Linux makes the record after the copy.** `filemap_read` calls `file_accessed` once its loop has ended (READ, `linux/mm/filemap.c:2877`). `touch_atime` returns nothing; it takes freeze protection with a try-lock and returns if the file system is frozen, skips when the mount cannot be written, and ignores a failed update with the words "we really don't care" (READ TWICE, `linux/fs/inode.c:2246-2260`).
- **Most reads are not recorded.** The default is `relatime` (READ, `linux/fs/namespace.c:4104-4106`): the time is updated when it is older than the file's change or a day old (READ TWICE, `linux/fs/inode.c:2076-2088`). `noatime`, the inode flag `S_NOATIME` and `O_NOATIME` on one open each turn it off (READ, `linux/fs/inode.c:2211-2225`, `linux/include/linux/fs.h:2268-2272`).
- **`lazytime` keeps the time in memory and writes it on a timer.** The interval is 12 hours, and the comment gives 24 as the worst case (READ TWICE, `linux/fs/fs-writeback.c:54-64`). It is also written when the inode is written for another reason, on `fsync`, and on the last `iput` (READ, `linux/fs/fs-writeback.c:2597-2629`, `linux/fs/sync.c:184-185`, `linux/fs/inode.c:1991`). The tree does not say what a crash loses; by the code it is whatever was only in memory (INFERRED).
- **FFS sets a flag and lets the syncer write.** After a read on a mount that is neither `noatime` nor read-only, `ffs_read` sets `IN_ACCESS` (READ TWICE, `freebsd-src/sys/ufs/ffs/ffs_vnops.c:846-848`); the time is filled in later (READ, `freebsd-src/sys/ufs/ufs/ufs_vnops.c:146-189`) and the lazy sync writes the inode without waiting (READ, `freebsd-src/sys/ufs/ffs/ffs_vfsops.c:1531-1548`), on a delay of 30 seconds (READ, `freebsd-src/sys/kern/vfs_subr.c:286`). mount(8) calls the access time something "which is rarely ever important" (READ, `freebsd-src/sbin/mount/mount.8:238-244` (tree)).
- **Plan 9 does the same and offers the same switch.** fossil's `fileRead` calls `fileRAccess` after it has released its locks, which sets the time in memory and returns at once on a read-only file system or under `noatimeupd` (READ TWICE, `plan9/sys/src/cmd/fossil/file.c:1688-1697`); a flush each second writes it (READ, `plan9/sys/src/cmd/fossil/fs.c:787-797`). The switch is `open -a`, "primarily to avoid wear on flash memories" (READ, `plan9/sys/man/8/fossilcons:749-751`). 9front's cwfs has a `noatime` word (READ, `9front/sys/src/cmd/cwfs/dentry.c:12-23`), and its hjfs and gefs set the time at creation alone (READ, `9front/sys/src/cmd/hjfs/fs2.c:168`, `9front/sys/src/cmd/gefs/fs.c:1878`).
- **Postgres records a read the same way.** A scan's counters are kept in the process, flushed to shared memory after a commit at most once a second (READ, `postgres/src/backend/utils/activity/pgstat.c:44-50`, `:723-732`), written to disk only at a clean shutdown, and "all stats are discarded" after a crash (READ TWICE, `postgres/src/backend/utils/activity/pgstat.c:12-16`).
- **JuiceFS is the counter-example, and its default is off.** Its access time is written in a transaction of its own inside the read call, after the read (READ, `juicefs/pkg/meta/base.go:2182-2186`, `juicefs/pkg/meta/sql.go:6083-6107`), and a failure is a warning (READ, `juicefs/pkg/meta/base.go:2117-2122`). The default mode is `noatime` (READ TWICE, `juicefs/pkg/meta/config.go:60-63`).

What the spec does: a read's whole dealing with the store is `put_behind`, which awaits nothing and never raises (decision 13); rows of one key become one row and a session's use is touched at most once in five minutes (decisions 13 and 14); a process that dies loses its buffer (decision 13). INFERRED: this agrees on every point but three. An access time is advisory and nothing refuses an operation for want of one, where a lost stamp refuses a later change until one read (decision 14). The prior art writes on a timer, of 1 second to 12 hours, where the spec flushes as soon as its one task is free. And the prior art has no bound in rows and no rule for which rows go first, where the spec keeps 16,384 and drops the oldest.

## 2. Bookkeeping lives in the store it describes, under names the user's names do not reach

- **ext4.** Inodes 1 to 8 are reserved, 8 is the journal, and the first ordinary inode is 11 (READ TWICE, `linux/fs/ext4/ext4.h:313-323`). The journal is a "normal (but hidden) file within the filesystem" (READ TWICE, `linux/Documentation/filesystems/ext4/journal.rst:42-44`). An external journal device is an option, "as opposed to an internal journal, which uses a reserved inode" (READ, `linux/Documentation/filesystems/ext4/journal.rst:81-83`, `linux/fs/ext4/super.c:1783-1784`).
- **FFS.** The soft-updates journal is a file named `.sujournal` in the root directory (READ TWICE, `freebsd-src/sys/ufs/ffs/fs.h:819`), found by a lookup of that name (READ, `freebsd-src/sys/ufs/ffs/ffs_softdep.c:2890-2913`), and made by `tunefs`, which refuses when a file of that name is there (READ, `freebsd-src/sbin/tunefs/tunefs.c:945-951` (tree)). Other tools carry the reserved names: the `/tmp` cleaner skips `.snap` and `.sujournal` (READ TWICE, `freebsd-src/usr.sbin/periodic/periodic.conf:55-62` (tree)).
- **Plan 9.** fossil keeps its super block and labels in the same disk file (READ, `plan9/sys/src/cmd/fossil/dat.h:324-330`), and its formatter makes `active`, `archive` and `snapshot` in the root (READ, `plan9/sys/src/cmd/fossil/flfmt.c:441-443`); a client that names no tree gets `main/active` (READ, `plan9/sys/man/4/fossil:159-160`). Its users file is an ordinary file in the served tree, `/active/adm/users` (READ, `plan9/sys/src/cmd/fossil/9user.c:679-680`). What fossil keeps about itself, it serves as files.
- **SQLite.** `sqlite_schema`, `sqlite_sequence` and `sqlite_stat1` are tables in the user's file (READ, `sqlite/src/sqliteInt.h:1248-1250`, `sqlite/src/build.c:2952-2962`, `sqlite/src/analyze.c:14-23`). The prefix is the engine's: a user's `CREATE TABLE sqlite_x` is refused "object name reserved for internal use" (READ TWICE, `sqlite/src/build.c:1040-1044`, `:1073-1078`). An application marks its own format in the header, `application_id` and `user_version` (READ, `sqlite/magic.txt:4-19`).
- **Postgres.** The catalogs are tables in every database (READ, `postgres/doc/src/sgml/catalogs.sgml:10-14`), in a schema of their own, `pg_catalog`, and a schema whose name begins `pg_` is refused: "The prefix \"pg_\" is reserved for system schemas." (READ TWICE, `postgres/src/backend/commands/schemacmds.c:105-110`). A table named `pg_x` in a user's schema is advised against and not refused (READ, `postgres/doc/src/sgml/ddl.sgml:3757-3763`).
- **A store of another format is refused, whole.** ext4 fails the mount on an unknown incompatible feature (READ, `linux/fs/ext4/super.c:3620-3629`); fossil's super block of another version is an error (READ, `plan9/sys/src/cmd/fossil/pack.c:205-207`); a Postgres cluster of another catalog version is refused with "It looks like you need to initdb." (READ, `postgres/src/backend/access/transam/xlog.c:4552-4565`).
- **The frameworks.** Litestream makes two tables inside the application's SQLite file, `_litestream_seq` and `_litestream_lock` (READ TWICE, `litestream/db.go:1172-1182`). JuiceFS keeps its sessions beside its file rows, every table under `jfs_` (READ, `juicefs/pkg/meta/sql.go:461-469`, `:617-623`). LangGraph's checkpointer puts four unprefixed tables in whatever database it is handed (READ, `langgraph/libs/checkpoint-postgres/langgraph/checkpoint/postgres/base.py:47-69`). DBOS is the one that keeps apart by default: a system database of its own, in a schema `dbos` (READ, `dbos-transact-py/dbos/_dbos_config.py:25`, `:38`), with one table, `datasource_outputs`, in the application's database under the same schema (READ, `dbos-transact-py/dbos/_datasource.py:172-173`).
- **When it is made.** A file system's bookkeeping is made when the file system is made, by an operator's tool: `tunefs`, `newfs`, fossil's `flfmt`. LangGraph's Postgres saver needs `setup()` called "the first time" (READ, `langgraph/libs/checkpoint-postgres/langgraph/checkpoint/postgres/aio.py:99-100`) and DBOS migrates at launch (READ, `dbos-transact-py/dbos/_dbos_config.py:54`). LangGraph's SQLite saver and Litestream make their tables lazily (READ, `langgraph/libs/checkpoint-sqlite/langgraph/checkpoint/sqlite/__init__.py:138-141`, `litestream/db.go:1152-1153`).
- **What a version is for.** Every framework that keeps a version row migrates forward by itself: LangGraph (READ, `langgraph/libs/checkpoint-postgres/langgraph/checkpoint/postgres/aio.py:104-120`), DBOS (READ, `dbos-transact-py/dbos/_sys_db_sqlite.py:77-126`), JuiceFS (READ, `juicefs/pkg/meta/sql.go:804-822`). JuiceFS refuses a store newer than the client and a running client then exits (READ, `juicefs/pkg/meta/config.go:188-191`, `juicefs/pkg/meta/base.go:929-932`); DBOS tolerates one, as "a newer peer" (READ, `dbos-transact-py/dbos/_sys_db.py:1127`).

What the spec does: the kernel's tables are in the mount's own SQLite file and in the mount's own Postgres database and schema, under three prefixes (decisions 4 and 5); they are made at the first use (decision 8); a store of another format is refused and never migrated, and the files are still served (decision 8); no argument names another place (choice 3). INFERRED: same-store placement, a copy that carries the records, and refusing another format are the convention. Five things are not. The prefix is reserved by a lint in VFS and by neither engine, where SQLite and ext4 make their names unreachable. Postgres and DBOS use a schema of their own, where the spec shares the mount's. A file system makes its bookkeeping at format time, where the spec makes it lazily. ext4 keeps an option for an external journal, where the spec has none. And ext4 and fossil fail the whole mount on another format, where the spec refuses the records alone.

## 3. A change and its record: one transaction in a journal, two here

A journaling file system writes a change and the record of it as one transaction: that is what ext4's journal is for (READ, `linux/Documentation/filesystems/ext4/journal.rst:42-44`, INFERRED from the purpose it states). Two of the frameworks do the same in SQL. DBOS runs the user's function and inserts its `datasource_outputs` row in the same session before the commit, and refuses a session that could split them, because every statement "commits atomically with the step checkpoint" (READ TWICE, `dbos-transact-py/dbos/_datasource.py:126-133`, READ `:483-514`); a commit whose outcome is unknown is settled by reading that row (READ, `dbos-transact-py/dbos/_datasource.py:567-570`). JuiceFS writes its change-log entry inside the change's transaction (READ, `juicefs/pkg/meta/sql.go:1140-1143`). Litestream never joins an application's transaction (READ, `litestream/db.go:1667-1669`).

What the spec does: a record that goes with a change is written in a second transaction, after the change or before it as a claim, and never inside it, on either engine (decision 10). INFERRED: this departs from the journal and from DBOS, and the spec gives its reason: the change may be on another mount and a storage commits inside its own method. The spec does not name it a departure, and its expiry is in "Left as it is" without the word: one transaction for a change and its record on a Postgres root.

## 4. Expired state is removed by a job that somebody schedules

- **FreeBSD.** The cleaner of `/tmp` is a script that cron starts (READ, `freebsd-src/usr.sbin/cron/cron/crontab:16` (tree)), and it ships off: `daily_clean_tmps_enable="NO"` (READ TWICE, `freebsd-src/usr.sbin/periodic/periodic.conf:55-62` (tree)). Clearing `/tmp` at boot is off as well (READ, `freebsd-src/libexec/rc/rc.conf:686` (tree)).
- **Plan 9.** `/tmp` is each user's own directory (READ, `plan9/lib/namespace:42`) and the shipped crontab cleans none (READ, `plan9/cron/sys/cron:1-16`). fossil discards no snapshot by default: "By default, all three times are set to none", and then "temporary snapshots are not automatically discarded" (READ TWICE, `plan9/sys/man/8/fossilcons:1080-1087`). An operator sets a lifetime, or runs `snapclean`, "a one-time event rather than a recurring event" (READ TWICE, `plan9/sys/man/8/fossilcons:1044-1046`), or `epoch`, "a very low-level way to retire snapshots" (READ TWICE, `plan9/sys/man/8/fossilcons:989-993`). Once a lifetime is set, a timer inside the server does the work (READ, `plan9/sys/src/cmd/fossil/fs.c:1031-1051`), and a snapshot "may grow to an age of almost twice the timeout" (READ, `plan9/sys/man/8/fossilcons:1060-1064`).
- **Linux, where it reaps by itself.** Keys are collected from a timer (READ, `linux/security/keys/gc.c:21-28`, `:51-67`), and NFSv4 leases by a work item that queues itself again (READ, `linux/fs/nfsd/nfs4state.c:7030-7040`). In both the expiry does not wait for the collector: a key past its time is refused `-EKEYEXPIRED` on use (READ TWICE, `linux/security/keys/permission.c:113-119`).
- **SQLite.** Nothing is reclaimed until asked: auto-vacuum is off unless turned on for a database (READ, `sqlite/src/btree.h:24-30`), and its incremental mode reclaims a number of steps when the application runs the pragma (READ, `sqlite/src/pragma.c:849-866`).
- **Postgres.** Reclaim is in the background by default: the autovacuum launcher is "an always-running process" (READ, `postgres/src/backend/postmaster/autovacuum.c:7-13`). Its own state with no lifetime stays until removed by hand: a replication slot keeps its log with no limit unless one is configured (READ, `postgres/src/backend/utils/misc/guc_parameters.dat:1313-1321`, `:2152-2160`).
- **The frameworks.** LangGraph's checkpoints never expire and go only by an explicit call (READ, `langgraph/libs/checkpoint-postgres/langgraph/checkpoint/postgres/__init__.py:386-417`); its store's lifetime "Defaults to `None` (no expiration)" (READ TWICE, `langgraph/libs/checkpoint/langgraph/store/base/__init__.py:563-575`), its reads filter expired rows (READ, `langgraph/libs/checkpoint-postgres/langgraph/store/postgres/base.py:271`), and its sweeper runs only if the application starts it (READ TWICE, `langgraph/libs/checkpoint-postgres/langgraph/store/postgres/aio.py:113-117`). JuiceFS and DBOS reap in the background and elect one runner an interval, by a counter row or an advisory lock, in batches of 1,000 (READ TWICE, `juicefs/pkg/meta/base.go:996-1008`; READ, `dbos-transact-py/dbos/_workflow_commands.py:105-108`, `:198-213`). Litestream enforces retention on a ticker (READ, `litestream/store.go:86-92`).

What the spec does: nothing is removed until the public `sweep` is called, a record past its expiry reads as absent before that, a record with no expiry is never removed, a session has none by default, and any caller allowed to sweep the root's trash runs the pass with no leader (decision 17). INFERRED: this is the convention of Unix and Plan 9 on every point. It departs from the systems that reap by themselves once a lifetime is set (fossil's timer, the Linux collectors, autovacuum, JuiceFS, Litestream), and the spec states its reason. A deployment that never schedules the call keeps every expired job, which is FreeBSD's shipped default for `/tmp` and is the price of the convention.

## 5. One writer, and what the others do

- **Plan 9 refuses at once.** A second open of an exclusive-use file "draws an error" (READ TWICE, `plan9/sys/man/5/stat:124-130`); the client retries, upas for five minutes at 500 ms (READ, `plan9/sys/src/cmd/upas/common/libsys.c:148-175`). A server may break a lock idle for minutes; cwfs, fossil and hjfs use five (READ, `9front/sys/src/cmd/cwfs/all.h:33`, `plan9/sys/src/cmd/fossil/9excl.c:22`).
- **Unix waits without a bound unless told not to.** `flock` sleeps unless `LOCK_NB` is given (READ, `linux/fs/locks.c:2233-2235`), with no time limit (READ, `linux/fs/locks.c:2142-2155`); without it the answer is `EAGAIN` and the caller retries (READ, `linux/fs/locks.c:1176-1185`).
- **SQLite has one writer in WAL and polls for it.** "There can only be a single writer active at a time" (READ, `sqlite/src/wal.c:3697`). The busy handler sleeps 1, 2, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 25, 25, 50, 50 and then 100 ms a turn until the timeout (READ TWICE, `sqlite/src/main.c:1740-1760`). It is a poll with no queue, and the tree states no order among waiters (READ, a search of `sqlite/src` and `sqlite/doc` for "starv" found one hit, about another mode). A transaction that read and then writes is refused at once with no wait (READ, `sqlite/src/btree.c:3750-3771`), which is why a writer begins as one.
- **What others do about the poll.** JuiceFS takes a lock of its own in the process before every write transaction on SQLite, with the comment "sqlite only allow one writer at a time" (READ TWICE, `juicefs/pkg/meta/sql.go:1349-1354`), and retries a busy error up to 50 times (READ, `juicefs/pkg/meta/sql.go:1358-1375`). DBOS sets the timeout to 30 seconds, because 5 "slow disks can exceed under load" (READ TWICE, `dbos-transact-py/dbos/_sys_db_sqlite.py:42-49`). Litestream advises the application to set 5 seconds (READ, `litestream/_examples/library/README.md:29-32`).

What the spec does: on SQLite the store's writes and the mount's share the file's one writer; a writer begins with `BEGIN IMMEDIATE`, waits 5 seconds a try for four tries, and is then refused as unavailable; rows written behind are tried again by the store itself (decisions 4, 9 and 15). INFERRED: taking the writer at the begin is what the engine requires. The bounded wait is neither Plan 9's refusal at once nor Unix's wait without end, and the retry by the store on a caller's behalf has no counterpart in what was read. The poll's lack of order is the fact the probes of the review measure: `../probes/output.md` has a sweep's second pass that got no turn until the first had finished.

## 6. What `close` owes to what is not yet written

- **A Unix `close` writes nothing that was delayed.** It gives up the descriptor, calls the file's `flush` if it has one, and returns that value (READ TWICE, `linux/fs/open.c:1492-1507`; READ `:1456-1474`); ext4 has no `flush` (READ, `linux/fs/ext4/file.c:985-986`), and the documents send a program to `fsync` to learn its data arrived (READ, `linux/Documentation/filesystems/vfs.rst:713-717`).
- **NFS is the exception that flushes, and it returns the error.** "Flush writes to the server and return any errors" (READ TWICE, `linux/fs/nfs/file.c:141-156`).
- **A closed descriptor is gone, error or not.** Plan 9: "Even if the clunk returns an error, the fid is no longer valid." (READ TWICE, `plan9/sys/man/5/clunk:30-35`), and `close` "is guaranteed to close it; there will be no error" (READ TWICE, `plan9/sys/man/2/open:132-136`).
- **The shutdown of a file system does flush.** fossil's `fsClose` stops its flusher and writes its metadata (READ, `plan9/sys/src/cmd/fossil/fs.c:139-146`). Litestream's `Close` "flushes outstanding WAL writes", retries for up to 30 seconds, and returns its error (READ, `litestream/db.go:900-930`). JuiceFS flushes its counters, removes its session row and waits for its goroutines (READ, `juicefs/pkg/meta/base.go:1026-1050`). SQLAlchemy's `dispose` closes only the connections that are checked in (READ, `sqlalchemy/lib/sqlalchemy/engine/base.py:3175-3183`).
- **SQLite removes its side files at the last close of all.** The closer tries for an exclusive lock, checkpoints and deletes the log (READ TWICE, `sqlite/src/wal.c:2520-2546`). Another connection in any process, a read-only closer, or a checkpoint that fails leaves the files (READ, `sqlite/src/os_unix.c:2030-2041`, `sqlite/src/wal.c:4326`). Which of two connections closes last does not matter.

What the spec does: the kernel's `close` flushes the buffer, ends the task and disposes the store's engine; a failed flush is logged and not raised; a close cut short can be finished by a second `close`; no public call flushes the writer (decision 19, and the ruling recorded under "Choices for the owner"). INFERRED: the right likeness is the unmount of a file system and not the `close` of a descriptor, and on that likeness the flush agrees. Three things depart. A failed flush is not returned, where NFS and Litestream return theirs. A second `close` may finish the first, where a closed descriptor is gone. And there is no `fsync`: Unix gives a program a call that waits for what was delayed, and the spec gives it `close` alone.

## 7. What the frameworks do with connections

DBOS gives its system database an engine of its own, 20 connections and no overflow (READ, `dbos-transact-py/dbos/_dbos_config.py:472-478`), and caps its polling at half that pool so that it cannot starve its own control plane (READ, `dbos-transact-py/dbos/_sys_db.py:1041-1048`); its row in the application's database rides the application's engine (READ, `dbos-transact-py/dbos/_datasource.py:223-228`). LangGraph's saver opens one connection, or uses a pool the caller hands it (READ, `langgraph/libs/checkpoint-postgres/langgraph/checkpoint/postgres/aio.py:57`, `:86-88`). JuiceFS has one engine for everything (READ, `juicefs/pkg/meta/sql.go:541-547`). SQLAlchemy's queue pool keeps 5, opens 10 more at a peak, and waits 30 seconds for one (READ, `sqlalchemy/lib/sqlalchemy/pool/impl.py:75-77`).

What the spec does: a second engine with the mount's pool arguments on both engines (decisions 4 and 5). INFERRED: a pool of the bookkeeping's own is DBOS's choice for the database that is wholly its own, and nobody read doubles an application's pool by default. No convention of Unix or Plan 9 bears on this; it is settled by the measurement in the review.

## What this memo does not settle

- **Whether a record should have a path.** It records that Plan 9 gives its servers' own state one and that the spec does not. The experiment that would settle it is a read-only view of sessions and jobs under `/.vfs`, which every one of the nine specs leaves out.
- **`tmpfiles`.** `systemd` is not cloned. The experiment is to clone it, read its licence, and read `tmpfiles.d`'s age rules against decision 17.
- **What a crash loses of an access time, in the words of the systems themselves.** Neither tree says it; the memo gives what the code implies. The experiment is to read mount(8) of util-linux, which is not cloned.
- **Whether the bounded wait should be longer or queued.** DBOS's 30 seconds and JuiceFS's lock in the process are read here; which serves VFS is a measurement, and the review's probes give the first figures.

## Proposed text: a numbered decision

To be added to `../spec.md` after decision 27, as the vid spec carries its decision 20.

> ### 28. Where this spec leaves Plan 9 and Unix it says so, with the constraint that moved it
>
> Article 3 of the constitution has VFS follow a convention of Plan 9 or Unix wherever one exists, and requires a deviation to be a numbered decision that names the principle, the deviation, the motivating constraint and the expected expiry. This is that decision. The section "Prior art, and where this spec departs" lists each point where a convention exists, what this spec does, and for each departure the four things the Article asks for. The conventions are those of a file system keeping its own bookkeeping: where it lives, when the record of a read is written, who removes what has expired, how a second writer waits, and what `close` owes.
>
> Reason: a person who knows `noatime`, a journal inode, `cron` and `fsync` should be able to predict what the kernel's records do, and where they cannot, the place to learn why is the spec that decided it.

## Proposed text: the section

To be added to `../spec.md` before "Earlier decisions this reverses".

> ## Prior art, and where this spec departs
>
> Read on 2026-11-23 in the clones and at the lines the research memo `research/2026-11-23-where-a-system-keeps-its-own-records.md` records.
>
> Where this spec agrees:
>
> - **Bookkeeping lives in the store it describes.** ext4's journal is inode 8 of the file system it protects, FFS's is a file in the root directory, and SQLite's and Postgres's own tables are in the user's file and the user's database. The kernel's tables are in the root storage's own file and its own database (decisions 4 and 5), and a copy of the store carries them.
> - **The record of a read is made after the read, in memory, by a call that cannot fail the read.** That is `touch_atime` in Linux, `IN_ACCESS` in FFS and `fileRAccess` in fossil. It is `put_behind` here (decision 13).
> - **Most reads leave no new record.** `relatime` writes a time only when it is stale, and a day apart at most. Rows of one key become one row here, and a session's use is written at most once in five minutes.
> - **A crash loses what was only in memory.** `lazytime` holds a time for up to a day, and Postgres discards its statistics after a crash. A process that dies here loses its buffer.
> - **A store of another format is refused and not repaired.** ext4 refuses an unknown feature, fossil another version of its super block, and Postgres another catalog version. Decision 8 refuses another `format_version`.
> - **Expired state is removed by a job that somebody schedules, and nothing is removed until then.** FreeBSD's cleaner of `/tmp` is a `cron` script that ships turned off, Plan 9 ships none, and fossil discards no snapshot until an operator sets a lifetime. Decision 17 removes nothing until `sweep` is called.
> - **What has expired reads as gone before it is removed.** A Linux key past its time is refused on use before its collector runs. Every read of a table that expires filters on the expiry.
> - **Nothing expires unless a lifetime is set.** A file does not, and a fossil snapshot does not by default. A session does not.
> - **Reclaim is a privileged act.** The `cron` job runs as root and fossil's commands are console commands. The pass runs only for a caller allowed to sweep the root's trash.
> - **One writer, taken when the transaction begins.** That is SQLite's rule for a file in WAL, and every store transaction that writes begins as a writer.
> - **The shutdown of a file system flushes.** fossil's `fsClose` writes its metadata before it ends. The kernel's `close` writes what the store had buffered.
>
> Where this spec departs. Each row is a deviation under Article 3.
>

## One line

The kernel store keeps its records where a file system keeps its journal, writes a read's record the way Unix writes an access time, and reclaims the way `cron` does; its eleven departures are each defensible, seven are already reasoned in the spec, four are not stated as departures or lack their reason, and none is yet a numbered decision under Article 3.

## The author's questions, answered first

1. **On SQLite the records are in the mount's own file (decision 4). Is the price complete, and is every failure a delay or a refusal and never a loss?** No to both (EXECUTED, [output](../probes/output.md) sections 1 to 4). What holds: no read waited in any window, no file was lost, both first touches work in either order and at once, and close leaves one file in either order. What the price leaves out is finding M1: a record written with a change can be lost for good, rows written behind are dropped once a writer is held longer than the buffer lasts, and a sweep pass takes most of the writer while it runs.
2. **How much worse is one file than the study's two shapes?** With nothing else running, hardly at all: a read's median is 21.9 ms against 21.4 with no store, and a one-file write's is 52.0 ms against 49.1 (EXECUTED). The store itself is slower to write: 17 flushes a second against 115 in a file of its own, a store commit waited up to 0.56 s to begin against 0.03 s, and the buffer held up to 213 rows against 7. Beside a 10,000-entry write, which holds the writer 8 s on this machine, no flush and no record failed; the buffer held 1,503 rows against 12 and the slowest flush took 7.75 s against 0.61 s. Behind a writer held 30 s, which is the length of the study's batch, one flush failed after 20.9 s in each hold, the buffer reached 12,210 of 16,384, and 1 of 7 one-file writes lost its record; in a file of its own nothing failed and the buffer held 10. The study's own script on this machine, with its one wait of five seconds, failed 1 of 14 flushes and 2 log writes in the mount's file and none in its own (section 5); so four tries and a flusher that flushes when free remove the failures at an 8-second batch and bring them back at a 30-second one.
3. **Is anything lost?** Yes, in two ways, both named by the spec as things that may happen and neither named as a loss in decision 4. A `record_after` that waits out 20 s is a record lost for good: once in three holds of 30 s (EXECUTED), and for spec 5 that is a change a rewind will not put back (READ, [spec 5](../../2026-11-22-a-rewind-puts-a-sessions-files-back/spec.md), decision 4, "a rewind will not put that change back"). And a writer held 60 s at 439 reads a second dropped 9,047 rows of 26,348 (EXECUTED, section 2).
4. **Does a pool of the store's own on Postgres earn its connections (decision 5, choice 2)?** Yes, at the default size, and 32 buys nothing (EXECUTED, sections 13 and 14). With 32 writing sessions a record adds 22 to 25 ms to a write's median of 192 ms, the same on the mount's pool, on a pool of 15 and on one of 32; the store's pool never held more than 8 connections. Beside 32 batches of 10,000, which exhaust the mount's pool, the record on the mount's pool failed 5 times and a flush once, each after the pool's 30 seconds, and on a pool of its own nothing failed and it held 4 connections at most. The study's suspect for its +151 ms, a log pool of 15 that was too small, is refuted: the pool of 15 was never more than 8 deep.
5. **Is removal by the public `sweep` alone sound (decision 17)?** As a convention, yes: it is how `cron` and Plan 9 do it (READ, the memo, section 4). Three things it leaves unanswered are findings M3, L2 and part of M1: a root that cannot be swept, a pass that ends early beside a rival, and a pass's hold on SQLite's writer.
6. **Is a root in memory with no argument for another place sound (decision 6, choice 3)?** Yes (INFERRED). One case has no answer, a durable mount added after the first record (finding L9).
7. **"Not checked", item by item.** The store in the mount's file: run, answers 1 to 3. ## Scale and the production posture

- The buffer: grows with reads through sessions, bounded at 16,384 rows, oldest dropped. Reached in 37 s of a held writer at 439 reads a second (EXECUTED).
- A flush: at most 1,024 rows, each statement cut by the live parameter budget. Holds.
- `insert_rows`, `upsert_rows`, `row_chunks`, `key_chunks`: cut by `rows_per_statement` and `membership_budget`, never a number of the spec's own. Holds; `bulk_insert` ran from a session of no storage on both engines (EXECUTED).
- `delete_expired` and `delete_where`: the text and binds do not grow, and one call removes at most its limit (EXECUTED, 3 binds on SQLite and 2 on Postgres at 12,000 rows).
- A sweep pass: 5,000 rows to a commit and no cap on the commits. Bounded in memory and interruptible between chunks. On SQLite the chunks follow each other with no room for another writer (finding M1).
- A statement of fixed binds: its rows are not bounded by the store, and the spec says so and gives the bound to the owning spec. Not run at size; "Not checked" names it.
- A 10,000-entry write through a session: its record of 20,000 rows was one transaction of 1.17 s on SQLite and 1.72 to 1.81 s on Postgres (EXECUTED), three to five times the third of a second the Cost section estimates (finding M2).
- The size of the store: unbounded by ruling, since a session has no lifetime by default. That is not a designed cap and the working agreement asks for none; the Cost section does not state it (finding M2).
- Both engines: every decision has its Postgres counterpart, and each claim about Postgres that the spec lists as read was run.

## Premortem

The spec was built as written and failed. The three likeliest reasons: a SQLite deployment closes a process while another process's batch holds the file, the store's last flush waits 20 s against a `close_timeout` of 10 s, and the store is never closed, with its side files left beside the database (finding H1). A Postgres deployment serves under a role that may not create tables, or with a default isolation of REPEATABLE READ, and every record call reports a fault in VFS or is refused under load (findings M4 and M5). And a production root masks `sweep` from its agents, or nobody schedules it, and the jobs table and the sessions grow for a year with no call that removes a row (findings M3 and M2).

## Findings, most severe first

1. **H1. A close cut short by its timeout does not keep the store, and on SQLite that is the ordinary close beside a batch** ([decision 19](../spec.md)). The spec says a close cut short "by its timeout or by a cancellation, keeps the store, as it keeps a storage (`base.py:559-565`)". Today's kernel keeps a storage only when the kernel's own `close` is cancelled: `asyncio.wait_for` turns a timeout into an exception the gather collects, and `_stranded_disposals` is emptied after the gather (READ, `src/vfs/base.py:569-574`). EXECUTED, [output](../probes/output.md) section 8: a storage whose close outlived `close_timeout` was entered once by the first `close()` and never again by the second, and never finished. A flush behind a held SQLite writer waits 20.9 s (EXECUTED, section 1) and the default `close_timeout` is 10 s, so with one file a process that closes beside another process's batch meets this by default. Consequence if built: the store's task and engine outlive `close()`, the rows left are lost with no warning, and `-wal` and `-shm` stay beside the database for as long as the process lives (EXECUTED, section 6, case `store-never`), which the landed close specs promise cannot happen. Fix text, for decision 19: "The store ends its own close inside the kernel's timeout and never relies on a second `close()` after a timeout. At close a flush makes one try and not four, and on SQLite that try's `busy_timeout` is the smaller of 5,000 ms and half of what is left of `close_timeout`. A flush that fails then ends the flushing, the rows left are dropped and counted with one `WARNING`, the task ends, and the engine is disposed. A close that is cancelled keeps the store, as it keeps a storage, and a second `close()` finishes it. A close that timed out is not kept, as a storage's is not (`base.py:569-574`)." With the test `test_close_behind_a_held_writer_still_disposes_the_store`: the file's writer is held by another connection, 3 rows are put behind, and `close()` issues 1 `BEGIN IMMEDIATE` on the store's engine, logs 1 `WARNING`, and leaves 0 store connections open; when the holder closes, the directory holds 1 file. The other way to close it is to change `AsyncVirtualFileSystem.close` so that a target whose close timed out stays in `_stranded_disposals`, for storages too; that is a change of today's behaviour and the spec would name it under "Earlier decisions this reverses".
2. **H2. Eleven departures from Unix and Plan 9, and no numbered decision for any** ([decisions 1, 4, 8, 10, 13, 14, 15, 17, 19](../spec.md)). Article 3 requires a deviation to be "a numbered Decision in the spec that makes them, naming the principle, the deviation, the motivating constraint, and the expected expiry". The spec has no such decision and no section on prior art; the vid spec gained both on the same day. READ, [the memo](../research/2026-11-23-where-a-system-keeps-its-own-records.md), section 8: the eleven are the stamp that refuses where an access time is advisory, the flush with no timer, no external place, names reserved by a lint and by neither engine, tables made at first use, a mismatch that refuses the records alone, a record with no path, two transactions where a journal has one, no timer for reclaim, the bounded wait with the store's own retry, and `close` with no `fsync` beside it. Seven are reasoned in the spec and none is named a departure or given an expiry. Four are not: a record with no path is reasoned from Article 1 and never against Article 3; the stamp's refusal is stated as a consequence only; the absent `fsync` is stated as ruled, with no reason; and the names reserved by a lint are not stated at all. Consequence if built: the Maintainers page is written from a spec that does not say where a person who knows `noatime`, a journal inode, `cron` and `fsync` will be surprised. Fix text: the decision 28 and the section "Prior art, and where this spec departs" that close the memo, pasted as they stand.

4. **M1. Decision 4's price is not complete, and one of its failures is a loss** ([decision 4](../spec.md), [The problem](../spec.md)). The problem says the store is built so that every failure "is a delay or a refusal and never a loss", and the price says "Every such failure is a delay or a refusal". Five things were measured that the price does not say (EXECUTED, [output](../probes/output.md) sections 1 to 4). The one read that calls the store, rule 3 of decision 22, now waits on the mount's writer. And in the quiet state the store commits 17 times a second where it committed 115, with waits of up to 0.56 s, and under 32 writers a record waited up to 3.4 s. Closes with: these bullets in the price, in place of "Every such failure is a delay or a refusal". "**A record that goes with a change can be lost, and not only delayed.** A `record_after` that waits out its four tries returns its warning and the change stands with no record, for good. A stamp lost so costs the session one refusal that one read clears. A change row lost so is a change a rewind does not put back (spec 5, decision 4). Behind a writer held 30 seconds this happened to 1 one-file write of 7." "**Rows written behind are dropped when a writer is held longer than the buffer lasts.** A flush behind a held writer fails after about 21 seconds and keeps its rows; at 439 reads a second the buffer was full after 37 seconds, and a 60-second hold dropped 9,047 rows." "**A sweep pass takes most of the writer while it runs.** Its chunks follow each other, and a writer that waits polls for the file. Beside a pass of 600,000 rows a one-file writer landed 17 writes in 9 seconds, against 102 beside a pass in another file; none was refused. A long pass is run when the root is quiet." "**The one read that calls the store waits on the mount's writer.** A read that a policy stops stores its job with `commit`, behind a batch on the root for up to four tries." "**With nothing else running the cost is small.** A read's median did not move, a one-file write's moved from 49 to 52 ms, and the store wrote 17 batches a second where a file of its own wrote 115." And The problem's sentence becomes "so that every one of those failures is a delay or a refusal, or a loss that decision 4 names".

6. **M3. A root that cannot be swept has no call that removes an expired record** ([decision 17](../spec.md)). The pass runs "only when the storage's retention succeeded". `sweep` is masked by `deny_ops` on a mount (READ, `src/vfs/base.py:258-267`, `src/vfs/ops.py:101`), it needs `read_write` (READ, `src/vfs/ops.py:174`), and its own docstring calls it "a developer's verb, meant to be left off the tools an agent is given" (READ, `src/vfs/base.py:1138`). A root whose mount masks `sweep`, whose permissions are read-only for the caller, or whose storage declares none, never runs a pass, and `store.sweep()` is private. Consequence if built: the deployment that follows the docstring's advice by masking `sweep` keeps every expired job for good and is told nothing. Closes with: a bullet in decision 17, "**A root that cannot be swept.** Where the root mount masks `sweep`, where its storage declares none, or where the caller may not write the root's trash, no pass runs and no other call removes an expired record.

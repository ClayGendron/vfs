## Findings, most severe first

1. **H1 — High (contract: a signed criterion fails on three engines; scale: the docstring's plan promise does not hold; adversarial: executed on SQLite, read from the lead's server plans) — criterion 7 fails on Postgres, MariaDB and SQL Server, and its world holds 50,001 rows awaiting the index where it says one** (`tests/support/plan_store.py:157` and `:172` to `:191`; `src/vfs/storage/backends/database/scope.py:308` to `:310`). Two facts, then what they mean. First, the world. EXECUTED with `p6_plan_store.py`, the support module's own `build_plan_store` and `overlay_readings` on a scratch SQLite file: after the build's reindex, 50,000 of the 50,200 content rows are `encoded = 0` with `indexable = 0`; their one-character bodies (`content="x"`) are never encoded, so they stay in the `NOT encoded` overlay for good, and after the overlay readings the count is 50,001. The module docstring and the Design say "one file written since the index pass". So D, the rows the dirty-row seek returns, is 50,001, nine tenths of the content table. Second, the plans (LEAD, read from `leg_postgres.log`, `leg_mariadb.log` and `leg_mssql_2.log`). Postgres plans the whole caller's statement as `Index Scan using ix_vfs_entries_path` with the rest as a filter, estimated 44,726 rows, and the partial caller's as a `Bitmap Heap Scan` over a `BitmapOr` of one `Bitmap Index Scan` per rights unit (`ix_vfs_entries_everyone_path` on `everyone_level >= 2`, then `ix_vfs_entries_path` on each granted root), estimated 238 rows, sorted on `path` under the limit. MariaDB plans the whole caller's as `type: index` on `ix_vfs_entries_path`, a walk of the whole index in path order, and the partial caller's as `index_merge` with `sort_union(ix_vfs_entries_path,ix_vfs_entries_owner_id)`, rows 803. SQL Server plans the whole caller's as a `Clustered Index Scan` reading 56,203 rows and the partial caller's as a full `Index Scan` of `ix_vfs_entries_everyone_path` (56,203 rows read) with key lookups. Oracle passes. SQLite passes because `unlikely()` holds the seek whatever the statistics say. So on three engines the partial statement is driven from the caller's grant ranges, which is exactly what the docstring says it never is ("never a join to the caller's visible entries"), and two whole-caller plans are full walks, which the Design says must be "written up the same day as a placeholder memo or a spec". For this world those plans are rational, INFERRED: with D at 50,001 the grant ranges (300 roots, about 8,400 rows) are the cheaper way in. The criterion cannot tell a good plan from a bad one here, because its world contradicts its premise. What it means at the reference deployment, INFERRED from these plans and labelled so: with the rights group in the WHERE, a planner without a rarity hint chooses among three accesses. (a) The `(encoded, kind)` seek visits D rows and evaluates the group on each; it costs D, about 50,000 by day and 1,000,000 by night, plus every never-indexable content row, which this world shows is a standing part of D. (b) Driving from the grant ranges (Postgres's `BitmapOr`, MariaDB's `sort_union`) visits every row under the caller's ranges, encoded or not, R of them, then filters `NOT encoded`; the bitmap loses path order, so `ORDER BY path LIMIT limit + 1` cannot stop it early and every match is sorted. It is cheaper than (a) when R is smaller than D: a caller of 300 small folders during the nightly batch (R about 3,000 against D of 1,000,000). It is ruinous when R is large and D small: a caller whose grants cover a department, R of 2,000,000, just after the daily rebuild with D in the hundreds, visits 2,000,000 index entries and heap rows per statement, per packed group, on about 150,000 calls a day; the planner takes it whenever its statistics overstate D, after a nightly batch before the next analysis, or when never-indexable rows hold the `NOT encoded` share up. (c) The `ORDER BY path LIMIT limit + 1` adds a third access, the path index walked in order with everything else a filter, stopping at `limit + 1` matches; it is what Postgres and MariaDB chose for the whole caller here. It is cheap when matches are dense and ruinous when the planner believes them dense and they are sparse, up to the whole path index, 10,000,000 entries, per statement; the rights group makes a partial caller's matches sparser than a whole caller's, so (c) is riskier for the partial caller; with the limit at 25,000, the planner's cost for (c) undercuts (a) whenever it estimates more than about 0.25 % of the table to match. Closes with: (1) the overlay readings run over a world where D is what the criterion says, for example the plan store's files given bodies the index encodes, or a store of their own for the overlay readings, so that after the reindex the count of `NOT encoded` content rows is 1, with an assertion in `overlay_readings` that it is; (2) the four server legs read again on that world, and the plans of the whole and the partial caller recorded in the Landing notes; (3) if a partial plan still leaves the seek with D at 1, a statement shape that fixes the access on the engines without a rarity hint (the dirty rows selected first, behind a fence the planner keeps, such as a Postgres `MATERIALIZED` common table expression or a MariaDB derived table it does not merge), decided in the spec or written up as a placeholder memo the spec waits on; (4) the docstring's last sentence made true of what the code controls, for example: "On SQLite the rarity hint holds the dirty-row seek, so the groups are residual filters on the rows it returns; on other engines the planner may drive the statement from the caller's ranges instead." The Design's rule on a whole caller's full scan applies to the MariaDB and SQL Server readings once (1) shows whether they survive a world with few dirty rows.

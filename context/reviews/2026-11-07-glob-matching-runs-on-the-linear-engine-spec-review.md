## The author's questions, answered first

1. **Is the 4 MiB gate and 5 MiB build pair sound, and is the argument that a refused pattern can match no path correct, including character classes and brace arms?** The pair works for wildcards, literals, `**` and brace arms, and the argument fails for character classes. EXECUTED: the spec's own shapes measure as it says (`"?" * 5_000` needs 5,000,128 bytes, past the gate and under the build limit; `"*?" * 1_024 + "*"` needs 2,090,128, under the gate; `"?" * 6_000` needs 6,000,128, past the build limit), so criteria 3 and 5 hold. Brace arms are fine, because the gate runs per arm and arms are capped at 64 (READ `glob.py:142`). But a class is one consuming atom whose compiled size grows with its member count, not with the path. EXECUTED: one class of every second code point from U+0100 to U+07FF costs 33,408 bytes; a pattern of four components, each 127 such classes, fails a 4 MiB build, and when built with a larger limit it matches the valid 1,020-byte path of four 254-byte segments of `Ā`. One class of every second code point up to U+10FFFF alone fails both limits and matches a one-character name. So the gate refuses patterns that do match stored paths, and the defect text "no stored path is long enough to need it" would be false for them. This is H2. One sound alternative exists: the minimum match length of each component, which `regex-syntax` exposes, against `MAX_SEGMENT_LENGTH` (255 bytes) refuses exactly the components that match no segment, whatever their class sizes.
2. **Does replacing byte-for-byte stdlib parity with verdict parity lose a guarantee?** Yes, two things, and both can be kept cheaply. READ `tests/pattern_matching/test_glob.py:558-584`: identical regex text meant identical verdicts on every subject, at fuzz depth 6. Verdict parity pins verdicts only on the subjects in the battery, and the spec drops the verdict check from depth 6 to depth 4. EXECUTED: depth 1 to 6 holds 125,378 defect-free patterns, depth 1 to 4 holds 2,654 (the spec says 2,800), and the subject battery of criterion 8 holds 585 strings. So the depth-5 and depth-6 class shapes (for example the inverted-range merges across three chunks) lose their verdict pin, and every subject longer than three characters loses it too. The three dialect rewrites are mechanical, so a textual pin can survive them; L3 gives the sentence.
3. **Is the worst-case residency in the Scale paragraph acceptable under the production posture?** Not as written, because it is per pattern, and a call holds many patterns. READ `src/vfs/params.py:251` and `params.py:262-263`: `globs` and `globs_not` are sequences with no length limit, so the number of arms one call compiles has no bound but the request. EXECUTED: eight arms held at once, each just under the gate, add 50 MiB resident, about 6 MiB per arm, above the spec's 5 MiB; building one such arm peaks at about 15 MiB. ## Scale and the production posture

Per row: one match per arm. The spec's statement that matching is linear in the path and that "nothing grows with the product of the pattern and the path" is false. The `regex` crate's worst case is the compiled size times the subject length. EXECUTED on one 1,024-byte subject: `*a` repeated 12 times costs about 2 µs a match, 500 times about 37 µs, and 1,000 times about 145 µs. All of those pass the gate. INFERRED: a 64-arm brace pattern of such arms against 10,000 candidate rows is about 90 seconds on the event loop. That is far better than today's unbounded stall, but it is not "no arrangement of wildcards can stall the event loop" (M2).

Per call, builds: the Scale paragraph says one build per arm per call, and the live tree builds far more. READ `base.py:2369` compiles the same glob once per scope root; `base.py:2260` compiles once per root row per arm (its own comment says so); `residuals` runs once per entry, root and arm (`base.py:2381`, `base.py:2467`, `base.py:2474`). The gate also builds a full regex per arm at every admission, and a glob call admits at the router (`base.py:1333`), in storage (`reads.py:396`) and in the prefilter (`pathterms.py:138`). EXECUTED: a small matcher build costs 58 to 104 µs, where today the second compile of the same text is an `re` cache hit. A 10,000-root `paths` batch is a supported call, so this is linear growth with a large constant (H3).

Per call, memory: grows with the total arms across channels, at about 6 MiB held per arm near the gate and about 15 MiB while building, with no bound on the pattern count (M1).

SQL: no statement changes; the prefilter (`pathterms.py`) is out of scope and untouched; no engine floor is involved.

Third round, per program: `(256 + 8) * words` words, 33 bytes a step, linear in the pattern as stated; EXECUTED `re.compile` of 100,000 `?` holds 0.87 MiB against 3.15 MiB of masks, about 3.6 times today, linear.

Third round, per match: the subject's characters times the program's words (decision 3), about 3 ns a word operation in the prototype (INFERRED from 147.9 ms for 10,000 rows of 1,000 characters at 5 words). The router's `pattern`, `globs` and `globs_not` carry no length limit (READ `params.py:245`, `params.py:308-309`), so one arm of 100,000 `?` costs about 1.6 million word operations per 1,024-character row, and 10,000 rows hold the loop for tens of seconds (INFERRED). That is a cost linear in the pattern, not a backtracking one, and the spec names matching on the loop as a profile with a direction; a bound on the active words takes the pattern's length out of it (L10).

Third round, per call: builds linear in roots times arms, as today, at a small constant (Question 13); matches linear in candidate rows times arms, on the loop until the next spec, as the Scale paragraph says; the cut's release consequence is not said (L9).

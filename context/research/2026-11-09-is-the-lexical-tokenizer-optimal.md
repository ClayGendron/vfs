# The lexical tokenizer is sound in its rules and a third slower than it need be in allocation, the engines it was compared with allocate nothing per token, the builder above it costs three times the tokenizer, and the lexical build's largest cost is not Rust at all but a Bloom filter SQLite rebuilds on every one of 1,809 scan pages since the planner-statistics landing, which three bounded shapes remove

On 2026-11-09, after [the dirty-row budgets memo](2026-11-09-the-dirty-row-budgets-of-grep-and-glean.md) and its kernel spike found that the engine tokenizer's own cost is allocation (a third of its 64 µs a body is the per-term `String` clone) and that a byte-level probe of the same rules ran at 24 µs, Clay asked that the tokenizer be researched "to make sure it is optimal". Three fresh researchers worked from [one brief](studies/2026-11-09-is-the-lexical-tokenizer-optimal/00-brief.md) against vfs at commit `b85e3df`, which fixed the tokenizer's rules and output and left free everything about how the output is produced: [study 01](studies/2026-11-09-is-the-lexical-tokenizer-optimal/01-how-mature-tokenizers-produce-terms.md) refreshed and read how tantivy, Lucene, bm25s, zoekt, pg_textsearch and Postgres's own parser produce terms, in about ninety minutes; [study 02](studies/2026-11-09-is-the-lexical-tokenizer-optimal/02-where-the-sixty-four-microseconds-go.md) attributed the tokenizer's time to its stages, measured six output-identical variants and a borrowed-lookup builder in pure Rust, and timed a full rebuild of the 100,000-file store to find the lexical build's share, in about two and a half hours; [study 03](studies/2026-11-09-is-the-lexical-tokenizer-optimal/03-the-lexical-builds-chunk-scan.md), commissioned when study 02 found half the rebuild outside Rust, measured the lexical build's chunk scan by timer on SQLite and Postgres, explained its plan from SQLite's own source, and measured nine bounded alternatives, in about three hours. This memo serves no intention; it feeds the specs section 7 names, and [the overlay kernel spec](../specifications/active/2026-11-09-the-overlay-counts-in-the-engine-and-the-scan-tier-shares-one-budget.md), "Fit to build", already carries the scanner refactor the tokenizer part of this memo points at. The memo commits us to nothing; the specs decide.

Labels: EXECUTED means a command ran in a study and the claim reports its output; READ means read in a file cited by path and commit; INFERRED means the researcher's reasoning from the two.

## 1. The answer in one paragraph

INFERRED from sections 2 to 6. The tokenizer's rules are right and its output stays; what is not optimal is how it produces that output, and the engines it was compared with show the shape: tantivy, Lucene and zoekt scan the text where it lies, hold one reused buffer per token, lowercase with a whole-token ASCII test first, and intern a term into an arena only the first time the corpus sees it, so their steady-state allocation per token is zero, where ours clones a `String` per occurrence, about a third of its 64 µs a body. Six output-identical variants were measured; the best combination runs at 34 µs a body with zero allocations, about 210 MB/s, and two of the plausible ideas did nothing while dropping the whole-body character vector was slower. But the tokenizer is about 7.5 per cent of a rebuild. The builder above it, which sorts and run-length counts every document's terms and then clones into its map, costs three times the tokenizer, and a borrowed-lookup accumulator that clones only a corpus-new term halves it with identical drained rows. And the lexical build's largest cost is not Rust: on the 100,000-file SQLite store, 35 of the lexical build's 61 seconds and 47 per cent of the 75-second rebuild go to a Bloom filter SQLite constructs over every entry row on every one of 1,809 page executions, a plan the planner chooses only because the store has `ANALYZE` statistics, which vfs gathers since the statistics landing of `2d263fb`, and whose cost is pages times entries, quadratic in the corpus; at the reference deployment's entry and chunk counts the shipped shape would take about 145 hours per rebuild. Three bounded shapes that keep the SQL Server rule remove it with identical epoch rows, the smallest a scalar subquery that is itself a regression on Postgres, the one good on both engines a drained set of qualifying entry ids with the chunks paged alone. Postgres has no such problem: its scan is 7.9 s at the driver's floor, and its lexical build is the builder and the inserts.

## 2. What the mature tokenizers do, and what ours does differently

READ in study 01, sections 2 to 7. tantivy's `SimpleTokenizer` iterates `char_indices` over the `&str`, emits one `Token` the tokenizer owns and the stream borrows, fills its text by `push_str` into a reused `String`, lowercases with `make_ascii_lowercase` after a whole-token `is_ascii` test, gates length by bytes, and its indexer hashes term bytes into an arena that copies a key only on first sight. Lucene's `StandardTokenizer` is a JFlex DFA over a streaming 255-character `char[]` window, its term a reused `CharTermAttribute` and a reused UTF-8 `BytesRefBuilder`, its `LowerCaseFilter` with an ASCII fast path and its own JMH bench, its `WordDelimiterGraphFilter` the one identifier splitter among them, emitting parts as integer offsets into one saved copy, and `BytesRefHash.add` interning once. zoekt scans bytes with an ASCII branch into a `uint64` trigram and allocates no string at all. The designs that allocate per term are the non-engines: bm25s runs `re.findall` over a lowered copy and makes a `str` per token, and Postgres's parser, which pg_textsearch delegates to, decodes the whole text to a wide-character array and allocates three times a word, pg_textsearch adding a fourth copy. Only bm25s publishes a throughput figure; tantivy, Lucene and zoekt ship benches that count tokens, allocations or bytes per operation and report no number in-file.

READ in study 01, section 8. Ours differs in six ways, every one of them about production and none about rules: it collects the whole body into a `Vec<char>` (only Postgres does this); it clones a `String` per emitted occurrence, freed again by the builder's sort-and-count unless the term is new to the corpus; it counts characters on every term for a floor of two, which only a two-to-four-byte term can fail; it tests ASCII per character inside the fold rather than per token; it binary-searches range tables of 651 to 749 entries and a 1,530-entry casefold map for every non-ASCII character where Lucene uses a two-level constant-time map; and it returns a `Vec<String>` that the builder sorts and looks up by owned key and that the Python binding turns into a list of about 1,100 `str` objects a body, where the engines consume each token through a sink and intern once.

## 3. Where the 64 µs go, and which variants help

EXECUTED in study 02, section 3, pure Rust, a staged copy asserted identical to the engine on every body. Corpus A is 2,000 generated prose bodies of 7,167 bytes (engine 61.2 µs a body); corpus B is 943 of the repository's own source and prose files, 20,367 bytes mean, 0.55 per cent non-ASCII bytes (engine 181.8 µs). The shares are the same on both: the character-vector collection 7 per cent, run scanning and class checks 20 to 23, the identifier split 14, the fold 15 to 17, the length check 8, and the per-term `String` clone 34. Per token the costs are flat across corpora: clone 20 to 21 ns, scan 12 to 14, fold 10, split 8 to 9.

EXECUTED in study 02, section 4. All six candidates kept byte-identical output on every body of both corpora. Emitting through a closure that receives a borrowed `&str`, no clone: 41.3 µs a body and one allocation. The ASCII fast path for a whole ASCII run: 58.6 µs. The byte-first length check and the 128-entry ASCII class table: 61.0 and 62.1, no change, because the std ASCII predicates already are that table. Dropping the `Vec<char>` for `char_indices` over the `&str`: 71.4 µs, slower, because the vector is a 4 ns-a-token decode cache the scanner reads three times. The combination the profile justifies, the borrowed sink over the ASCII run path with the vector kept: 34.1 µs a body, zero allocations, 210 MB/s, 31 ns a token on corpus A, and 107 against 181.8 µs on corpus B; the same combination returning a `Vec<String>` as today's binding does is 57.4 µs, so the clone is the cost and the shape of the return decides whether it is paid.

INFERRED in study 02. Two of the prior-art differences are real costs here, the clone and the per-character fold test, one is a wash (the character count), one is a loss to remove (the vector), and the non-ASCII table lookups were not exercised enough to say (corpus B is half a per cent non-ASCII), which the last section names.

## 4. The builder costs three times the tokenizer

READ in study 02, section 5, and study 01, section 1. `LexicalAccumulator::add_doc` calls `tokenize`, takes the length, sorts the `Vec<String>`, run-length counts it into a reused vector moving each first-seen `String` in, then looks each `(term, tf)` up in a `HashMap<String, TermList>` and either pushes the posting or inserts the `String` as a new key; so every occurrence allocates once and only a corpus-new term keeps its allocation. The build's binding receives Python strings and tokenizes in Rust, so there is no Python-side token list in the build.

EXECUTED in study 02, section 5. The engine's `add_doc` is 181.5 µs a corpus-A body with 1,147 allocations, three times its tokenizer. A borrowed-lookup accumulator, an interner that clones only a corpus-new term and a pending slot per term list, drains identical rows at 91.8 µs a body and 68 allocations fed by the borrowed-sink tokenizer under SipHash, and 73.6 µs fed by the full combination under FNV-1a, a probe of what the hash costs on short keys and not a proposal. On corpus C, 20,000 chunks sampled from the store at 1,478 bytes, the shape the build actually feeds, 39.9 µs a chunk falls to 15.6 to 20.2.

## 5. What the rebuild pays, measured

EXECUTED in study 02, section 6, and study 03, section 2, on a copy of the 100,000-file SQLite store with one file rewritten so one live entry awaits the index, which rebuilds the whole gram and lexical index: `reindex()` 74.6 s in both studies, 2,463 to 2,475 statements. `build_lexical_epoch` 60.6 to 61.5 s, 82 per cent of the rebuild; inside it the builder's `add_docs` 17.1 to 17.6 s over 462,963 chunks (37.9 µs a chunk, matching the bench within 5 per cent), the lexical inserts 6.8 s, `finish` and the two drains 0.7 s, and the keyset chunk scan 35.1 s, confirmed by a timer around each page execute in two samples (35.06 and 35.12 s). The gram feed streamed the same 681 million characters in 2.8 s with a server-side cursor. The tokenizer's share, by the bench's ratio on store chunks, is about 5.6 s, 7.5 per cent of the rebuild and 9 per cent of the lexical build.

EXECUTED in study 03, section 3. The scan is 1,809 pages of 256 rows at a median of 19.08 ms, flat from the first page to the last, 377 KB of content a page. `EXPLAIN QUERY PLAN` shows the right join order, chunks by primary key then one unique-index probe per row into the entry table, no sort, no full scan, and one more step: `BLOOM FILTER ON vfs_entries`. `sqlite3_stmt_status` shows 916,385 virtual-machine steps a page against 1,544 for the same page without the join and 2,313 for the join without the entry filter, with `filter_miss=256, filter_hit=0`: the filter admits every probe and excludes nothing, because every chunk's entry qualifies, and about 914,000 steps a page go to constructing it over 103,921 entry rows. Ids-only costs the same 12.6 ms as ids with content, so the bytes are not the cost. READ in `src/where.c` at `3d17c3f9e`: `whereCheckIfBloomFilterIsUseful` sets the filter when the inner table has `ANALYZE` statistics, the lookup's terms are expected to cull, and the outer loops' estimated searches exceed the inner table's estimated rows, an estimate that ignores the `LIMIT`; `sqlite3ConstructBloomFilter` emits the construction behind an `OP_Once`, once per execution of the statement, so a page re-executed 1,809 times builds it 1,809 times. EXECUTED: with the store's `sqlite_stat1` rows deleted the plan is worse, entries outer with a temp B-tree sort per page at 68.5 ms, and with `ANALYZE` run again the filter plan returns; the statistics vfs gathers since `2d263fb` are what make the planner choose the filter, and they are not the thing to remove.

EXECUTED in study 03, section 4, nine alternatives each as a harness-side replacement of `_chunk_batches` with the shipped `build_lexical_epoch` around it, each asserting the same row counts and the same SHA-256 over every row of the four lexical tables as the baseline. Bigger keyset pages only amortize the filter: 12.65 s at 1,024 rows, 5.57 at 4,096, 3.67 at 16,384 with 24 MB of content held a page. The two-phase fetch, qualifying chunk ids drained once (4.9 s, 70 MB of id pairs) then content by primary-key membership, 2.09 s at the Oracle floor of 1,000 ids a probe and no faster at the dialect's 32,668. A streaming cursor with the `lex_docs` writes buffered to the end, 3.30 s and 139 MB of rows, the shape the docstring forbids on a driver without multiple active result sets. A temp table of qualifying entry ids, 97 s, because the join to it plans with a Bloom filter too and a temp table has no statistics. A Python set of the 100,000 qualifying entry ids drained once (0.82 s, 10 MB) with the chunks paged alone, 3.34 s. `entry_id IN (SELECT ...)`, 204 s, a full entry scan and a sort per page. A correlated scalar subquery compared to a constant, the one spelling the planner leaves unflattened, 3.64 s with the same statement count and no client state; `EXISTS`, `likelihood`, unary plus, `CROSS JOIN` and the filter in the `ON` clause all keep the Bloom plan.

EXECUTED in study 03, section 5, on the same 100,000 files built into Postgres 17. The shipped page plans as a nested loop over the chunk primary key with a memoized unique-index probe, no filter step, 0.3 to 0.7 ms of execution and 3.8 ms a page through the driver; the scan is 7.89 s, and the per-row driver cost of 10 to 17 µs for a 1.5 KB row is the floor every shape pays. The Python set prefilter is within noise (7.37 s plus 0.86 s to drain), the two-phase fetch slower (13.6 s, the 1,000-row probes at 15.6 ms each), and the scalar subquery a regression: Postgres cannot estimate a SubPlan filter, inflates its cost past `jit_above_cost`, and JIT-compiles every page for 5 to 7 ms, three times the shipped page's wall. The lexical build on Postgres is the builder (20 s) and the inserts (10 s), not the scan.

## 6. The projection

INFERRED in study 03, section 6. The filter's cost is not linear in the corpus: it is paid once per page and scales with the entry count, so at the reference's entry count it is about 1.34 s a page, and at the reference's chunk count the shipped 256-row pages number about 390,000, about 145 hours per rebuild; 16,384-row pages bring that to about 2.3 hours and hold 24 MB a page; any of the three filter-free shapes brings it to about 7 CPU-minutes a day. The reference deployment runs Postgres, where none of this applies and the build is the builder and the inserts; SQLite is the dev and test default and a store some deployments will run, and on it the shipped shape does not complete a day's rebuild in a day.

## 7. Synthesis

INFERRED, on the owner's question. The tokenizer is optimal in what it decides and not in how it is made: its rules match the reference tokenizer and are frozen into the index's fingerprint, and every engine studied makes the same decisions about where a word starts and how it folds; what the engines do differently is allocate nothing per token, and ours allocates once per occurrence. The fix is a scanner that hands each folded term to a sink as a borrowed slice, with `tokenize` kept as a cloning wrapper so the index's output and its fingerprint do not move, which is exactly the refactor [the overlay kernel spec](../specifications/active/2026-11-09-the-overlay-counts-in-the-engine-and-the-scan-tier-shares-one-budget.md) decision 3 already carries for the overlay; the ASCII run path rides with it when a spec takes it. Measured at the rebuild, that is 1 to 5 CPU-minutes a day at the reference scale, small, and worth doing because the scanner exists for the overlay anyway.

INFERRED, what the research found instead. The index build's Rust cost is the builder, not the tokenizer: sort-and-count over owned strings and a map keyed by owned strings, three times the tokenizer's time, and the interning shape every engine in study 01 uses halves it with identical rows, 17 to 21 CPU-minutes a day. That is a spec of its own, on the builder, standing on study 02's bench and pinned by the drained-row digests. And the lexical build's largest cost is a planner artefact the statistics landing created on SQLite: a filter rebuilt per page whose cost is quadratic in the corpus. That is the spec to write first, because it is a correctness-of-cost regression in a landed change and because at the reference scale the shipped shape does not finish. The shape is one of three, and the choice is between the smallest edit (the scalar subquery, one expression in one statement, a regression on Postgres unless spelled per dialect through the profile, which is the `DialectProfile` rule for a decision SQLAlchemy takes no position on) and the one spelling that serves both engines (the qualifying entry ids drained once into a set, the chunks paged alone by primary key, about 1.1 GB of ids at 11,000,000 entries, and non-covered chunks' content shipped across the driver on a store with many tombstoned entries); the two-phase fetch is the shape when a build must also skip chunk rows by id. The statistics stay: without them the page is worse still. The docstring's SQL Server rule, no cursor open while the build writes, is kept by all three and broken only by the streaming cursor, which is the fastest shape and the one the rule forbids unless the writes take a second connection.

INFERRED, the order the figures support: the chunk scan fix first, then the builder, with the tokenizer's scanner arriving through the overlay kernel spec and the builder spec taking the sink from it.

## 8. What this memo does not settle

Which of the three filter-free scan shapes to ship, which is the spec's decision; the experiment behind it is study 03's harness on a store with a third of the entries tombstoned, counting the rows and bytes the Python-set shape ships and discards against the scalar subquery's engine-side probes, and `EXPLAIN` of the scalar subquery on SQL Server, Oracle and MariaDB through the `db_test` legs.

Whether a later SQLite takes the `LIMIT` into the Bloom filter's estimate, which would make the shipped join cheap again: a read of `where.c` per release and a rerun of study 03's explain script on the pinned Python's SQLite.

The borrowed-lookup builder's memory (a pending slot per term list and an interner beside the lists) at the store's 131,296 terms, and the hash's probe lengths on an adversarial corpus: study 02's bench with peak resident size recorded and a colliding-term corpus.

The tokenizer's non-ASCII path, exercised on half a per cent of corpus B's bytes: the bench on a mostly non-ASCII corpus (Cyrillic, CJK, mixed-script identifiers) with the parity assertion kept, and Lucene's two-level map as the shape to measure against if the range searches show.

The Postgres build of a freshly written, unanalysed schema, whose first `reindex()` took 759 s against 80 s on analysed tables: `EXPLAIN` of the page and of the gram scan on a fresh schema before autovacuum, since a plan without statistics may be the no-statistics shape SQLite showed.

The run-to-run variance of `add_docs` (16.9 against 19.8 s by run order): the baseline and one variant run three times each, interleaved, with and without an idle minute between.

## One line

The tokenizer's rules are right and its allocation is a third of its cost, which the overlay kernel's borrowed-sink scanner removes with the output unchanged; the builder above it costs three times more and halves with the interning shape every engine uses; and the lexical build's largest cost on SQLite is a Bloom filter rebuilt on every scan page since the statistics landing, quadratic in the corpus and 145 hours per rebuild at the reference scale, which three bounded shapes remove, so the chunk scan is the spec to write first.

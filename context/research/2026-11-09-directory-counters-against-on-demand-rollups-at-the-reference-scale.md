# At the reference deployment's shape, a root-level rollup computed on demand scans every row under the root and costs 0.4 to 7.8 seconds per call at one million files and 7 seconds at ten million, while direct-children counters answer the same question from one row per directory in 50 to 600 milliseconds and cost one coalesced increment per touched directory on a write; recursive counters answer in milliseconds but touch three to five times as many rows per batch, land every write on the root's row, cut concurrent edit throughput by a fifth to a half on every engine, and on MariaDB's default snapshot isolation collide as restart conflicts

On 2026-11-09 Clay asked, after the aggregation prior-art memo named this experiment as the one to run before a spec chooses between maintained counters and on-demand rollups, "Can you run the experiment?", then set the scale: ten million files, a thousand users, the daily and nightly change rates and the index cadence that the scale research turned into the reference deployment in AGENTS.md, with the remark that "aggregations on each write seem like a lot of extra overhead". This memo reports that experiment, run by the coordinator on Clay's Apple M1 Pro MacBook Pro (10 cores, Darwin 25.5.0) against SQLite in a file and against Postgres 17, MariaDB 11.8, SQL Server 2025 and Oracle 23ai in the Docker containers the storage conformance suite uses, at one million files on every engine and at ten million files on Postgres. The box was two hours of coordinator time across the afternoon, interrupted by three harness defects named in section 7; work was paused by Clay before two legs ran, the SQLite ten-million leg and the MariaDB contention rerun, and section 7 and the last section say so. Every figure below comes from a result file under [the studies folder](studies/2026-11-09-directory-counters-against-on-demand-rollups-at-the-reference-scale/experiments/results/). The harness is [rollup_experiment.py](studies/2026-11-09-directory-counters-against-on-demand-rollups-at-the-reference-scale/experiments/rollup_experiment.py) and it touches none of vfs's tables or code. This memo answers the unsettled item of [the aggregation memo](2026-11-09-how-file-systems-and-search-tools-shape-aggregation-apis.md) and stands on [the reference deployment memo](2026-11-09-what-an-enterprise-scale-deployment-looks-like.md) for its shape and its multipliers. It commits to nothing; the spec that adds the rollup columns decides.

INFERRED means this memo's reasoning from the two. Every wall-clock figure is a fact about this laptop and these containers, not a benchmark, and the counts beside it are the measurement.

## 1. The answer in one paragraph

EXECUTED, every figure from the result files. The synthetic tree matched the reference shape on every engine: a two-file median directory, a ten-file mean, 29 to 30 percent of directories holding no files, a largest directory of 2.3 to 2.7 percent of the corpus, mean depth 5.4 with 16 percent of directories at depth 8 or more and a maximum of 14. The on-demand rollup at the root scanned every row under it, 1.1 million at one million files and 11 million at ten million, and cost 0.4 seconds on Oracle, 0.7 on SQL Server, 1.0 on Postgres and SQLite and 7.8 on MariaDB at one million files, and 7.2 seconds on Postgres at ten million. The direct-children counter read scanned one row per directory, 100,000 at one million files and 1,000,000 at ten million, and cost 49 to 597 milliseconds at one million and 330 milliseconds on Postgres at ten million. The recursive counter read scanned the root's children only, 1,223 rows at one million and 12,071 at ten million, in 2 to 218 milliseconds. On the write side a spread 10,000-file batch touched 6,590 directories at one million files and 9,165 at ten million; direct counters cost one coalesced increment per touched directory, 7 to 10 statements, and 11 percent (Oracle) to 290 percent (MariaDB) of the entry insert's own time; recursive counters touched 22,045 and 43,844 rows, 3.3 to 4.8 times as many, in 2.7 to 13 times the time. A concentrated 10,000-file batch into one directory touched one counter row under either design. A single edit added one statement and one row under direct counters and one statement touching depth plus one rows under recursive counters. Under 16 concurrent writers editing in separate transactions, direct counters cost nothing measurable against the baseline on any engine, while recursive counters, which land every edit on the root's row, cut throughput by 20 percent on Postgres at one million files, 33 percent at ten million, 42 percent on Oracle and 45 percent on SQL Server, and on MariaDB 11.8 collided as error 1020 snapshot-isolation restarts that exhausted twenty retries per edit, so MariaDB's contention row is a failure rather than a rate. INFERRED against the reference deployment's multipliers: the on-demand root rollup is ruled out as a verb by the per-call multiplier (150,000 calls a day against a 7-second scan of 11 million rows); direct counters fit the per-write multiplier (one bounded, uncontended increment per touched directory, about a million a day) and keep the read bounded by directories rather than files; recursive counters buy a 6-to-60-fold cheaper read at the price of a serialization point on the root and a restart storm on MariaDB, which is the price the file systems in [the scale memo](2026-11-09-what-an-enterprise-scale-deployment-looks-like.md) section 5 refused to pay for every directory.

## 2. The tree and the harness

EXECUTED. Directories are built first, their depth drawn from Poisson(4.4) plus one and clipped at 14, each parent drawn from the level above; files are assigned to directories by lognormal weights with sigma 1.8, so a directory holds a median of 2 files and a mean of 10 with a long tail; file sizes are lognormal with a 4 KB median. At one million files the tree had 100,000 directories, a largest directory of 27,439 files, 29.6 percent of directories with no files, mean depth 5.4, 15.6 percent at depth 8 or more, and 1,223 children of the root; at ten million, 1,000,000 directories, a largest of 23,362, 28.9 percent empty, 15.7 percent at depth 8 or more and 12,071 children of the root. INFERRED: the root fan-out is wide because the Poisson depth puts 1.2 percent of directories at depth one; a real mount root has tens of children, not thousands, which makes the recursive read here a harder case than the reference and leaves the other two shapes untouched.

EXECUTED. The tables are the experiment's own: `exp_entries` with a unique path, an indexed parent path, kind, depth, size and lines; `exp_dir_direct` with one row per directory that has children, holding direct-children files, dirs, bytes and lines; `exp_dir_recursive` with one row per directory holding its subtree's totals and an indexed parent path. Inserts go through SQLAlchemy's `insertmanyvalues` at the dialect's page (2,000 rows on four engines, 349 on SQL Server under its 2,100-parameter cap); counter updates are `x = x + :delta WHERE path = :p` executemany statements chunked at 1,000 rows, the JuiceFS flush size; `IN` lists are chunked at 1,000, Oracle's cap. Statements are counted by a `before_cursor_execute` listener, rows by the parameter sets bound, and the rows a read must scan are the rows under the root in the table it reads.

## 3. Reads: the root and a mid-tree directory

EXECUTED. The root rollup at depth one, as `tree(max_depth=1, columns={"total_bytes"})` would ask it, on each engine at one million files, with the rows scanned and the milliseconds:

| Engine, corpus | On demand (rows under root) | Direct counters (directory rows) | Recursive counters (root's children) |
|---|---|---|---|
| SQLite, 1M | 1,100,000 rows, 980 ms | 100,000 rows, 57 ms | 1,223 rows, 2 ms |
| Postgres, 1M | 1,100,000 rows, 1,021 ms | 100,000 rows, 87 ms | 1,223 rows, 3 ms |
| MariaDB, 1M | 1,100,000 rows, 7,787 ms | 100,000 rows, 597 ms | 1,223 rows, 8 ms |
| SQL Server, 1M | 1,100,000 rows, 743 ms | 100,000 rows, 154 ms | 1,223 rows, 218 ms |
| Oracle, 1M | 1,100,000 rows, 406 ms | 100,000 rows, 49 ms | 1,223 rows, 24 ms |
| Postgres, 10M | 11,000,000 rows, 7,235 ms | 1,000,000 rows, 330 ms | 12,071 rows, 53 ms |

EXECUTED. Postgres's plan for the on-demand root read at ten million files is a parallel sequential scan of `exp_entries` feeding a grouped aggregate on the computed child segment, 11,000,000 rows actual; at one million files a hash aggregate over a sequential scan of 1,100,000 rows. The mid-tree read, a depth-two directory holding about one percent of the files (6,900 rows under it at one million, 23,976 at ten million), cost 20 to 94 milliseconds on demand at one million and 358 milliseconds on Postgres at ten million, against 2 to 9 milliseconds for direct counters over 8 to 73 directory rows and 0.3 to 7 milliseconds for recursive counters over 3 or 4 rows.

INFERRED. The on-demand shape costs in proportion to the subtree, exactly as study 03 of the aggregation research predicted of every engine that counts by scanning: at the root it is the corpus, and the reference deployment's per-call multiplier makes a 7-second, 11-million-row scan per `tree` call unaffordable at any call rate an agent generates. The direct-counter read costs in proportion to the directories under the root, a tenth of the rows and a tenth to a twentieth of the time, which is affordable as a verb at ten million files on four engines and marginal on MariaDB at 597 milliseconds for one million; it grows with the directory count, so at the root of a ten-million-file mount it is a third of a second on Postgres and would be several seconds on MariaDB. The recursive read costs in proportion to the fan-out and is the only shape that stays in single-digit milliseconds, except on SQL Server, where the 218-millisecond figure for 1,223 rows is the one anomaly in the table and is recorded as measured.

## 4. Writes: the nightly batch, the ingest and the single edit

EXECUTED. A spread batch of 10,000 new files into existing directories drawn like the corpus (the ETL ingest contract), the entry insert's own cost beside each counter design's cost, at one million files and on Postgres at ten million:

| Engine, corpus | Entry insert | Direct counters: rows, statements, ms | Recursive counters: rows, statements, ms |
|---|---|---|---|
| SQLite, 1M | 313 ms | 6,590 rows, 7 statements, 80 ms | 22,045 rows, 23 statements, 177 ms |
| Postgres, 1M | 261 ms | 6,590, 7, 161 ms | 22,045, 23, 580 ms |
| MariaDB, 1M | 791 ms | 6,590, 7, 2,302 ms | 22,045, 23, 6,927 ms |
| SQL Server, 1M | 3,661 ms | 6,590, 7, 5,609 ms | 22,045, 23, 17,972 ms |
| Oracle, 1M | 745 ms | 6,590, 7, 84 ms | 22,045, 23, 1,115 ms |
| Postgres, 10M | 557 ms | 9,165, 10, 501 ms | 43,844, 44, 2,305 ms |

EXECUTED. The spread edit batch, 10,000 existing files re-sized (the nightly batch's shape), read 10,000 rows in 10 chunked `IN` selects and updated them in 10 statements, then touched 6,572 direct counter rows (9,242 at ten million) and 21,805 recursive rows (44,420), with the same ratios of time. The concentrated batch, 10,000 files into one directory, touched one direct counter row and two recursive rows (the directory and the root) in one statement each, 0.6 to 11 milliseconds on every engine. The single edit, the daytime agent write, is two statements as a baseline (a read and an update), one more statement touching one row under direct counters, and one more statement touching depth plus one rows under recursive counters (3 rows at depth 2, 6 at depth 5).

INFERRED. Three things the write table says. First, the coalescing works: 10,000 files became 6,590 to 9,165 direct increments and 22,045 to 43,844 recursive ones, never 10,000 ancestor chains, and the recursive design touches 3.3 to 4.8 times the rows of the direct one because a depth-5.4 tree shares ancestors. Second, the direct counters' cost relative to the entry insert ranges from 11 percent on Oracle to 290 percent on MariaDB and 153 percent on SQL Server, and the two expensive engines are the two whose drivers execute an executemany `UPDATE` as one round trip per row (aiomysql and aioodbc without `fast_executemany`), so the measured overhead there is the driver's row-at-a-time shape rather than the engine's work; a set-based increment joined to a values list is the future direction and was not measured. Third, the entry insert here is one row per file with no body, chunks or version, so in vfs's real write the counter increment is a smaller share of the whole than these ratios show; the counts (one increment per touched directory, chunked at 1,000) are the portable measurement, the milliseconds are this laptop's.

## 5. Contention: sixteen writers, separate transactions

EXECUTED. Sixteen workers each ran 50 single-file edits, each edit its own transaction, in three modes: the baseline read-and-update, the baseline plus a direct counter increment on the file's parent, and the baseline plus recursive increments on every ancestor including the root. Throughput in edits per second, with restarts counted where the engine refused a transaction as a conflict:

| Engine, corpus | Baseline | Direct counters | Recursive counters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Postgres, 1M | 1,579 /s | 2,097 /s | 1,252 /s |
| Postgres, 10M | 1,571 /s | 1,830 /s, 0 restarts | 1,051 /s, 0 restarts |
| Oracle, 1M | 1,876 /s | 1,760 /s | 1,092 /s |
| SQL Server, 1M | 525 /s | 560 /s, 0 restarts | 289 /s, 0 restarts |
| MariaDB, 1M | not completed, see section 7 | not completed | failed after 20 restarts per edit |

INFERRED. Direct counters are within the run-to-run noise of the baseline on every engine (faster on two, slower on one, by less than a quarter), because sixteen writers editing random files in a million-directory tree rarely share a parent. Recursive counters cost 20 to 45 percent of throughput on three engines at this concurrency, because every one of the 800 edits updates the root's row and the root's row lock serializes them; the reference deployment's peak of about 30 agent writes a second on one row is below the point where this probe bites, and its nightly 50 files a second in 10,000-file batches coalesces to one root increment per batch, so the contention cost at the reference scale is real but bounded, and it is the shape, not the number, that the file-system precedents refused.

## 6. The repair scan and the seed

EXECUTED. Building the direct counters from scratch with one `GROUP BY parent_path` over every entry row, which is also the strict read's and the repair verb's cost, scanned 1,100,001 rows in 1.3 seconds (SQL Server), 1.8 (Oracle), 2.0 (SQLite), 2.8 (Postgres) and 5.3 (MariaDB) at one million files, and 11,000,001 rows in 49.8 seconds on Postgres at ten million. Loading the ten-million-file tree took 462 seconds on Postgres through 5,512 insert statements. INFERRED: the repair scan is a whole-corpus operation and fits the reference deployment's per-corpus multiplier, once a day beside the rebuild, and never a per-call path; its cost is the on-demand root read's cost plus the write of one row per directory.

## 7. What went wrong in the harness, and what it says about vfs on these engines

EXECUTED. Three defects stopped legs and were fixed; each is a fact about running vfs on the engine as much as about the harness. On MariaDB, the first run failed because the counter update was written as raw SQL and `lines` is a reserved word there; the fix was to build every update through SQLAlchemy Core, which quotes it. On SQL Server, the first run failed because a `GROUP BY` on a computed child segment must be grouped through a subquery; the fix was a derived table. On the ten-million Postgres leg, the shared test container keeps its data directory in tmpfs inside an 8 GB Docker VM, and the VM reset under the load, taking every container with it; the fix was a throwaway Postgres container with a disk volume, which loaded ten million rows in 462 seconds.

EXECUTED. Two more are findings rather than defects. On SQL Server the recursive contention probe wedged for 52 minutes: sixteen workers queued on the root row's exclusive lock, and the two sessions holding locks sat idle with open transactions, because aioodbc runs every blocking ODBC call on the event loop's default thread pool, 14 threads on this ten-core machine, and all 14 were occupied by blocked updates, so the lock holders had no thread on which to commit; giving the connection a 64-thread executor cleared it. INFERRED: vfs serving SQL Server through aioodbc with more concurrent writers than executor threads can starve itself the same way under any hot row, and the executor's size is a configuration fact the storage layer should own. On MariaDB 11.8, whose `innodb_snapshot_isolation` is on by default under `REPEATABLE READ`, the second transaction to increment a counter row that another committed transaction changed after its snapshot fails with error 1020, "Record has changed since last read", rather than waiting for the lock; with recursive counters every edit hits the root's row, and twenty restarts per edit were not enough for sixteen writers to finish, which is the failure recorded in the first contention run. vfs's dialect profile already classifies 1020 as a restart. A rerun with a thousand restarts allowed, to measure how many a recursive counter design would pay, was prepared in the harness and had not run when work paused.

## 8. What the experiment says against the reference deployment

INFERRED, with the multipliers from [AGENTS.md](../../AGENTS.md) "The reference deployment". Per verb call, about 150,000 a day: the on-demand root rollup at 7 seconds and 11 million rows is out, and even the mid-tree one-percent directory at 358 milliseconds and 24,000 rows on Postgres is a scan the per-call rule forbids as a default; the direct-counter read at 330 milliseconds and a million rows at the root is affordable as a verb but not free, and the recursive read at 53 milliseconds is the only one that is cheap at the root. Per file write, about 1,050,000 a day: direct counters add one bounded increment per touched directory, no hot row, and no measurable contention at sixteen writers; recursive counters add depth-plus-one rows and a root-row serialization that already costs a fifth to a half of throughput at sixteen writers and surfaces as restarts on MariaDB. Per batch, 100 a night: both designs coalesce a 10,000-file batch to one statement per 1,000 touched directories, 7 to 10 statements for direct and 23 to 44 for recursive, and the concentrated batch to one. Per corpus, once a day: the repair scan at 50 seconds for ten million rows fits beside the rebuild. The shape the file systems at a billion files chose, direct-children counters on every directory and recursive totals only on marked roots, is the shape these numbers favour: the direct counters carry the write side at the cost the precedents accept, and a recursive row on the mount root alone, incremented once per batch and once per edit, gives the 53-millisecond root read without putting every directory's write on one row. That is a reading of the measurements, and the spec decides.

## What this memo does not settle

A set-based counter increment, one `UPDATE` joined to a values list instead of an executemany of single-row updates, would change the MariaDB and SQL Server write figures and was not measured; it is the experiment to run when the spec fixes the statement shape. The recursive read's 218-millisecond anomaly on SQL Server for 1,223 rows was not investigated; an execution plan from that engine is the experiment. The contention probe ran sixteen writers against one row for 800 edits; the reference deployment's thirty writes a second sustained for a working day against a mount root's row, with the engine's lock queue under a real transaction mix, is the longer probe that would size a single recursive root row. The harness inserts one entry row per file; a run through vfs's own `write` with bodies, chunks and versions would give the counter's share of a real write. The SQLite ten-million leg was stopped during its load when work paused, and the MariaDB contention rerun had not started; both are one command each in `run_remaining.sh` and the result files they would write are absent. No real-engine run was made with vfs's isolation pins (`REPEATABLE READ` for ops on MariaDB), and whether a counter increment should run at `READ COMMITTED` to avoid error 1020 is for the spec and a test on that engine.

## One line

On demand scans the corpus and is out as a verb; direct-children counters cost one bounded increment per touched directory and read in tenths of a second from one row per directory; recursive counters read in milliseconds but serialize every write on the root and restart on MariaDB, so they belong on marked roots, not on every directory.

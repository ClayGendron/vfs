# Grep and glean already brute-force the same dirty rows in the same order, a dirty row costs grep about 10 µs and glean about 175 µs whatever the store size, glean's 500 was declared where grep's 25,000 was measured and every reference-scale cadence exceeds it within minutes, the gap between the two verbs is Python tokenizing around the Rust tokenizer and not SQL, and a kernel that keeps the tokenize-and-count in the engine takes glean to about 60 µs a row with identical hits

On 2026-11-09, after the directory-flag spec landed and before the refresh spec is built, Clay asked whether glean could "run brute force like grep does on dirty rows", or whether there is "a specific limit we should set for grep and glean so that, if more than N number of files are dirty, they won't be included in the results", and whether "we have different rules for different sizes file systems"; the aim, in Clay's words, was "to confirm we have things setup properly with defaults that make sense and are in alignment with each other". Prior art was dropped from the plan at Clay's decision, since the question is about our own numbers. Two fresh researchers worked from [one brief](studies/2026-11-09-the-dirty-row-budgets-of-grep-and-glean/00-brief.md) against vfs at commit `b85e3df`: [study 01](studies/2026-11-09-the-dirty-row-budgets-of-grep-and-glean/01-the-budget-inventory-and-the-alignment-check.md) inventoried every runtime limit the two verbs apply on or around the scan tier from the code and the git history and checked the two for alignment, in about two hours; [study 02](studies/2026-11-09-the-dirty-row-budgets-of-grep-and-glean/02-the-dirty-set-cost-curve.md) built 10,000- and 100,000-file stores on SQLite and a 100,000-file store on Postgres, dirtied them at 500 to 25,000 rows, and measured both verbs with glean's overlay budget at its default and lifted to 25,000, in about two and a half hours. When the figures showed the whole gap between the verbs to be the overlay's tokenize-and-count in Python, Clay asked "could we design something better? Is it worth a research agent?" and a third researcher ran [study 03](studies/2026-11-09-the-dirty-row-budgets-of-grep-and-glean/03-the-overlay-counts-kernel-spike.md), a spike that benchmarked the engine's tokenizer in pure Rust, built a prototype kernel behind a scratch pyo3 module and reran glean through it on study 02's 100,000-file store, in about fifty minutes. This memo serves no intention; it feeds [the refresh spec](../specifications/active/2026-10-31-refresh-the-incremental-index-verb.md), whose decision 23 leaves both budgets as they are, and whatever spec the owner writes from section 6. The memo commits us to nothing; the spec decides.

## 1. The answer in one paragraph

INFERRED from sections 2 to 5. Glean already brute-forces the dirty rows exactly the way grep does: both verbs nominate the rows the index has not seen through one shared helper, in path order, with the same gates and scope, and read their live bodies; the only differences are that glean tokenizes and scores where grep regex-matches, and that glean stops at a fixed 500 rows where grep takes whatever is left of a measured 25,000-candidate pool after the index side has drawn from it, under a 32 MiB body-batch law and a deadline that reaches inside its matcher, neither of which the overlay has. The 500 is drift, not a decision: its own spec called it "not a measured optimum", the memo that shaped the lexical leg had asked for grep's budget and deadline, and at the reference deployment's write rate every cadence passes 500 dirty rows about five minutes after any index pass, so the overlay's warning is a constant and glean's ranking is always short of the files written since the last pass. Measured, a dirty row costs grep's scan tier 10 to 25 µs and about 7 KB of body and glean's overlay about 175 µs and the same 7 KB, the same on a 10,000-file store as on a 100,000-file one, with Postgres adding about 45 µs of body transfer to both; 145 µs of glean's share is the tokenize-and-count loop running in Python around the Rust tokenizer, and the SQL legs of the two verbs cost the same. Neither verb reached the 10 second wall inside its count budget on either engine, and when their counts agree they drop the same file, the dirty row whose path sorts last. So the rule should not key on store size, because the cost never did and the dirty set is set by write rate and cadence; the two verbs can share one count the day the overlay's tokenize-and-count moves into the engine, where the project's tooling rule already puts it, and until then the overlay's shape, not only its number, is what is out of line with grep. The spike measured that day: a fused kernel that tokenizes and counts the query terms in one Rust pass with no per-token allocation and no dictionary runs at 41 µs a body, the boundary crossing costs under 2 µs a document at either granularity, and inside glean on the 100,000-file store it took the overlay from about 165 to about 60 µs a dirty row with identical hit lists and scores on every query at every level, so glean brute-forcing the dirty set costs 2 to 2.5 times grep's scan tier instead of 7, and what remains of the gap is the engine tokenizer itself at 40 µs a body against a trigram-verified regex under 1 µs.

## 2. What is there today, and where each number came from

READ in study 01, section 1, whose table holds eighteen limits with module, line, record and landing commit. Grep's scan tier is bounded in three shapes: by count, `CANDIDATE_BUDGET = 25_000` minus the index candidates, with the scan tier skipped and a record saying "with the unindexed overlay not consulted" when the index side alone fills the pool; by bytes, bodies fetched and matched in `CONTENT_BYTE_BUDGET = 32 MiB` batches by exact stored size; and by wall, `WALL_TIME_BUDGET = 10.0 s` from the verb's entry, consulted between ladder stages, between arm chunks of the nomination, before every content batch and inside the matcher, whose budget is cut at worker start. Glean's overlay is bounded in one shape: by count, `OVERLAY_BUDGET = 500` whatever the index side did; it fetches every admitted body in one statement chunked only by the engine's membership budget, and its tokenize-and-score hop is offloaded with no deadline argument and reads no clock.

EXECUTED in study 01 with `git log -S` and `git show`: the 25,000 was 10,000 at spec 093 and 097 and rose to 25,000 under spec 141 on the strength of spec 103's sweep over 10k, 25k, 50k, 100k and 200k candidates at about 75 µs each, where eight of nine truncated rows un-truncated by 25,000; the 32 MiB came in under spec 097 after a measured 251.7 MB resident for a one-row result, the budget itself declared; the 10 s was declared under spec 093 and made a constructor knob under spec 097 by Clay's call; the 500 arrived with the lexical leg under spec 132 and has not moved. READ: spec 132 line 94 says "`SCOPE_ID_BUDGET = 5,000` and `OVERLAY_BUDGET = 500` are declared, refereed constants, not measured optima; the ranking harness has no scoped queries yet to tune them against"; [the glean memo](2026-08-26-glean-in-the-engine.md) line 136 recommended the overlay be "bounded like grep's scan tier by the same candidate budget and deadline". The one later measurement to touch the 500, the baseline study of 2026-10-31, saw the record appear at 501 dirty rows and did not lift the cap.

READ in study 01, section 2b: both verbs' truncation records share the kind, the warning severity, the sentence shape "at the <reason>; <guidance>" and the wall cause string; the reasons are "candidate budget" against "overlay budget", the guidance differs by the word "pattern" against "query", and glean's overlay record carries `{"scanned", "budget"}` where every grep record carries no data. Neither record reports how many dirty rows were left out, because the nomination statement is `LIMIT limit + 1` and never counts the rest, and neither names a dropped path.

READ in study 01, section 2e: a content write stamps the row not chunked and not encoded; the vector leg's statement requires `chunked` and a non-null embedding, and the embed step runs only inside the index pass; the overlay produces lexical-only hits. A dirty entry is ranked by one leg and an indexed one by two, the fusion renormalises over the legs present, and no record says so.

## 3. What a dirty row costs, measured

EXECUTED in study 02, sections 4, 5 and 9, median of three runs per level, the store opened with the wall lifted so the curve is visible and once more with the default wall so the records a caller sees today are on file. On SQLite, grep's scan tier, isolated by a pattern that nominates no index candidate, cost 17 to 25 µs per dirty row and about 7 KB of body read, split about 70 per cent body fetch, 25 per cent the nomination statement and under 3 per cent the matcher; the slope of the whole five-pattern suite was 10 to 13 µs per dirty row per call. Glean's overlay with the budget lifted to 25,000 cost 155 to 180 µs per dirty row and the same 7 KB: nominee fetch 5 to 7 µs, surrogate-id fetch 8 µs, body fetch 9 to 12 µs, tokenize-and-count 135 to 146 µs, scoring under 0.2 µs. A micro-measurement on 2,000 bodies split the tokenize-and-count into 87 µs of the Rust `tokenize` at 83 MB/s and 65 µs of the Python `Counter`. The statement count of either verb does not move with the dirty set on SQLite, and the per-row figures were the same on the 10,000-file and the 100,000-file store to within noise.

EXECUTED in study 02, section 8, on Postgres over the 100,000-file store at 10,001, 23,401 and 25,001 dirty rows: grep's scan-only pattern 65 to 66 µs per row, of which 56 to 58 µs is body transfer at about 120 MB/s through asyncpg; glean's lifted overlay 210 to 225 µs per row, of which 144 to 150 µs is the same tokenize-and-count and 53 to 55 µs the body fetch; the same statement counts as SQLite, one each for the nominee, surrogate and body fetches of the overlay and three to ten 32 MiB batches for grep. The Postgres leg ran its levels compounding on one store because a second copy did not fit the container's 3.9 GB tmpfs, which moves the index side's share between levels and not the scan tier's per-row cost; the dirty count in every reported row is exact.

EXECUTED in study 02, section 7: neither verb reached the 10 second wall inside its count budget on either engine. The slowest grep call was 1.02 s on SQLite and 2.7 s on Postgres, the index side of a common word on the large store; the slowest glean call with the lifted budget was 4.56 s on SQLite and 9.4 s on Postgres at 25,000 dirty rows, the latter carrying a fixed 3.6 s index-tier cost for a query of two very common terms that is absent on SQLite and independent of the dirty set. INFERRED from the slopes: grep would need 400,000 to 600,000 scan rows to reach 10 s, sixteen to twenty-four times its pool; glean at 175 µs would cross at about 57,000 dirty rows on SQLite, and on Postgres the lifted 25,000 and the wall meet within 0.6 s on that one query. The count budgets, not the wall, are the operative limit on both verbs.

EXECUTED in study 02, section 4: on the 100,000-file store a common word's index side nominated 24,872 of the 25,000 candidates at every dirty level and left the scan tier 128 rows, with the bare "candidate budget" record and no data; the record's "overlay not consulted" variant fires only when nothing at all is left. A dirty file holding a common word is in grep's answer on that store only if its path is among the first 128 dirty paths. At 25,000 dirty rows every one of the five patterns carried the record, the index side having shrunk as rows left the index.

## 4. What is dropped, and whether the two verbs drop the same thing

EXECUTED in study 02, section 6: a sentinel file written last, holding a unique term and sorting last by path, was searched for at every level by both verbs. Grep found it at 501, 2,001, 5,001 and 10,001 dirty rows and lost it at 25,001, nominating 25,000 of the 25,001; glean at the default budget lost it at every level including 501, because the budgets are inclusive and the sentinel was the 501st path; glean at the lifted budget found it up to 10,001 and lost it at 25,001 with `{scanned: 25000, budget: 25000}`. The same held on Postgres. READ in study 01, section 2c: `entries_for_scan` orders every nomination statement by path, merges by path and prunes to the lowest `limit + 1`, and both verbs pass it the same gates, exts, scope roots and scope facts, so the two cut the same ordered list and, once their counts agree, drop the same rows; while the counts differ, what glean drops is a superset of what grep drops.

INFERRED in study 01, section 2c: path order is collation over the whole stored path, so what is dropped is whatever sits under the directories whose names sort last, on every call, with no relation to when the file was written; at the reference shape an agent working under a late-sorting root is the one both verbs fail for until the next index pass. INFERRED in the same section, not measured in study 02's wrapped phases: the nomination statement sorts every dirty row's key regardless of the cut, so its cost grows with the dirty set and not with the budget; study 02 measured the nomination at 5 to 7 µs per dirty row on both engines, which is consistent with that shape and small beside the body fetch and the tokenizing.

## 5. The reference deployment

INFERRED in both studies from [AGENTS.md](../../AGENTS.md) "The reference deployment": about 50,000 organic single-file writes a working day, 100,000 at the ceiling, is about 104 writes a minute over eight hours and 208 at the ceiling; the dirty set is that rate times the time since the last index pass, so it is about 1,250 to 2,500 rows under a 15-minute refresh, 5,000 to 10,000 under an hourly one, and tens of thousands by afternoon under three rebuilds a day with no refresh. The nightly million is indexed by the rebuild and does not add to the daytime set. A 100,000-file store with the same write rate has the same dirty set, and study 02 measured the per-row cost to be the same on 10,000 and 100,000 files, so neither the dirty set nor its cost depends on the corpus.

INFERRED from section 3 at those sets: a brute-force glean overlay at today's 175 µs costs a query 0.22 to 0.44 s and 9 to 17 MB of body under the 15-minute cadence, 0.9 to 1.8 s and 34 to 69 MB hourly, and 4.4 s and 172 MB at 25,000 rows, against grep's 0.03 to 0.06 s, 0.12 to 0.25 s and 0.63 s for the same bytes; with the tokenize-and-count in the engine, the overlay's row cost falls toward grep's 25 µs plus whatever the kernel takes for 7 KB, which the micro-measurement bounds at about 87 µs today for tokenizing alone at 83 MB/s and which a counting kernel would not exceed. The overlay's 500 is passed about five minutes after any pass at the centre and 2.4 minutes at the ceiling, so under every cadence glean's lexical leg scores 20 to 40 per cent of the dirty set at 15 minutes, 5 to 10 per cent hourly and 1 to 5 per cent with rebuilds alone, always the lowest-sorting paths, and every working-hours glean call carries the warning. Grep's pool holds the whole dirty set at the 15-minute and hourly cadences when the index side leaves the room, and loses it at rebuild-only cadence and on any common pattern at ten million files, where the index side alone fills 25,000.

## 6. The overlay with its tokenize-and-count in the engine, measured

EXECUTED in study 03, section 3, pure Rust over the 2,000 generated bodies of 7,167 bytes and 1,092 tokens each, median of seven passes: the engine's own `tokenize`, which returns a `Vec<String>`, runs at 64 µs a body and 112 MB/s, so the 87 µs study 02 saw through Python was 23 µs of materializing 1,092 Python strings a body; a dictionary of every token costs about 60 µs a body in Rust however it is built, the same order as Python's `Counter`, so moving the `Counter` to Rust without removing it would buy little; a fused kernel that tokenizes with the same rules and tables, hands each folded term to the counter as a borrowed slice, and compares it against the two to four query terms with no dictionary runs at 41 to 43 µs a body and 170 MB/s, with under 1 µs per extra query term; and a third of the engine tokenizer's own 64 µs is the per-term `String` clone, with a byte-level probe of the same rules on all-ASCII bodies reaching 24 µs. The kernel's token counts and term frequencies were asserted equal to the engine tokenizer's on every body and query before timing.

EXECUTED in study 03, section 4: called from Python on the same bodies, the batched kernel costs 40.6 to 42.0 µs a body, within a microsecond of pure Rust, and a call per document costs 0.6 to 2.4 µs more, so the pyo3 crossing is not a factor at either granularity. The status quo path reproduced study 02's figure at 143 to 148 µs a body.

EXECUTED in study 03, section 5, inside the verb on study 02's 100,000-file SQLite store with the budget lifted to 25,000 and `_overlay_blocks` routed through the prototype, fresh store copy per level, median of three passes: the overlay's tokenize-and-count fell from 139 to 144 µs a dirty row to 38 to 41, the overlay as a whole from 159 to 185 to 59 to 65, and the whole call's slope from 164 to 171 to 65 to 70 µs a row; the statement count stayed at 12 and the bytes moved were the same on both paths. On all five queries at 5,000, 10,000 and 25,000 dirty rows the two paths returned the same ten paths in the same order with a maximum score difference of zero, and the sentinel file was found at 5,001 and 10,001 and lost at 25,001 with the same record. At 25,000 rows a glean call's overlay drops from 4.1 s to 1.5 s on SQLite. INFERRED for Postgres from study 02's SQL legs of about 70 µs a row plus the kernel's 40: about 110 µs a row, an ordinary 25,000-row call near 2.8 s instead of 5.5, and the worst call near 6.8 s instead of 9.4, its 3.6 s of fixed index-tier cost untouched.

INFERRED in study 03, section 6: with the kernel, glean costs 2 to 2.5 times grep's scan-only pattern per dirty row on SQLite and under 2 times on Postgres, down from 7 and 3.3; both verbs clear the 10 second wall inside a shared count of 25,000 on both engines; the two read the same 7 KB a row and issue the same statements, so the remaining asymmetry is the engine tokenizer at 40 µs a body against grep's matcher under 1 µs, and the byte traffic of about 7 KB a dirty row is the shared cost one count limit bounds. At a 15-minute refresh and a dirty set of about 2,500 rows each glean's overlay costs 0.15 s of offload CPU with the kernel against 0.4 s today, and about 17 MB of body either way.

## 7. Synthesis

INFERRED. The two verbs are aligned where it matters least and misaligned where it matters most. They share the nomination helper, the order, the gates, the record's kind, severity and sentence, and the wall; their SQL costs per row are the same. They differ in the three things that decide what an agent sees: the count, 500 against the remainder of 25,000, which is drift by the verbs' own documents; the body residency, one unbatched fetch against a 32 MiB law, which at 500 rows is 2 MB at the median and 100 MB at the tail and grows linearly with any lift; and the deadline, which stops grep's matcher mid-batch and never reaches glean's tokenize-and-score hop. One difference is justified by structure: grep's scan share is what the index side leaves of a shared entry pool, glean's index side is a chunk depth with no pool to subtract from, so a fixed overlay count is glean's natural shape. One is justified by cost: the overlay is lexical-only, since covering the vector leg at query time would mean embedding every dirty body per call.

INFERRED, on the owner's three questions. Glean can brute-force the dirty rows as grep does, and already does in mechanism; what it cannot yet afford is grep's number, because each row costs it 17 times what it costs grep and 85 per cent of that is Python around the Rust tokenizer, which is the shape the tooling rule in AGENTS.md names for a kernel in `crates/vfs-core`, and the spike measured that kernel at 60 µs a row inside the verb with identical answers. One limit N past which dirty files are left out is what both verbs have today, and the measurements say the right N is the same for both once the per-row costs are within a small factor, as they are with the kernel, with the wall as the backstop neither reaches at 25,000 on either engine; if the kernel does not land first, a glean count sized to the refresh cadence the refresh spec enables, in the low thousands, costs under half a second a query at the median body and is the interim the figures support, while 25,000 at 175 µs is not. The rule should not differ by store size: the dirty set is set by write rate and cadence, the per-row cost was identical on 10,000 and 100,000 files, and a size-keyed budget would protect nothing that a count, a byte batch and a deadline do not already protect; where size does show is on the index side, where a common word fills grep's pool at ten million files and leaves the dirty set 128 rows, and that is a record wording and a pool-shape question, not a dirty-row budget.

INFERRED, the shape a spec would decide, each item from the sections above: the overlay counts kernel of study 03 in `crates/vfs-core`, taking a batch of bodies and the folded query terms and returning each body's token count and the term frequency of each term, with a pure-Python oracle in `tests/support/oracles/` pinning it over bodies with non-ASCII, mixed-case and underscored identifiers, so one shared count is affordable; the overlay batched by `CONTENT_BYTE_BUDGET` and its score hop carrying the deadline, grep's existing law, which the design-options study already named as hygiene independent of refresh; one count for both verbs' scan tiers with one record payload carrying `scanned` and `budget` on both, and grep's record saying how many rows the scan tier received when the index side took most of the pool; and the nomination order, path against recency or a stable hash, decided with the cost of a sort on a stamp measured first. The refresh spec's decision 23 would then stop saying the traits are unchanged and say what the overlay's budget is and why, or a follow-on spec would carry these and decision 23 would point at it.

## 8. What this memo does not settle

The kernel's per-row cost on Postgres is a projection from study 02's SQL legs plus study 03's kernel, not a measurement: the experiment is study 03's rerun driver pointed at the Postgres test container with a fresh schema at 10,000 and 25,000 dirty rows.

Whether the engine's own `tokenize` should drop its per-term `String` clone, a third of its 64 µs a body, for the index build's sake as well as the overlay's, and whether a byte path for ASCII runs is worth a second code path: the experiment is study 03's fused and byte-path variants run inside the full index pass on the 100,000-file store, counting rows visited and reporting the build wall as a fact, against the index-visit multiplier of about 11,000,000 a day.

The kernel's return shape, one tuple per posting against packed arrays per term, was in the noise at 25,000 rows and was not costed further: the experiment is the prototype with a `bytes`-returning encoder against the tuple form, counting Python objects created per call.

Whether a recency or stable random nomination order costs more than path order at the reference shape: the experiment is `ORDER BY path`, `ORDER BY version DESC` and a hashed order over the `(encoded, kind)` partition at 10,000 and 50,000 dirty rows on Postgres, counting rows visited and sort work.

Whether a truncation record can afford the dirty total: the experiment costs a count over the not-encoded content partition at 10,000,000 rows on Postgres against the per-call multiplier of about 150,000 calls a day.

How often a common pattern's index side alone fills grep's pool at 1,000,000 files, so the dirty set is left out and the record does not say so: the experiment runs the baseline study's ten patterns on a 1,000,000-file store and counts the records by variant.

The shape of a wall-truncated overlay, how many nominated rows are scored before the record and whether the last-path file is among them: the experiment is a run at 25,000 dirty rows with `glean_wall_seconds` at 1 s.

The fixed 3.6 s a two-very-common-terms query pays on Postgres in the index tier and not on SQLite: the experiment wraps the lexical statistics read, the head-block fetch and the tail-block statements and reports the posting bytes each moves per query on both engines.

The scan tier under a glob or scope channel, where the nomination fans into arm chunks and the statement count can grow: the experiment is study 02's levels under a globs channel admitting a tenth of the tree.

## One line

Both verbs already brute-force the same dirty rows in the same order and drop the same file at their cap; grep bounds that work by a measured count, a byte law and a deadline that reaches its matcher, glean by one declared count that every reference-scale cadence passes within minutes; a dirty row costs grep about 10 µs and glean about 175 µs on any store size, the difference being Python tokenizing around the Rust tokenizer; a kernel that keeps the tokenize-and-count in the engine was measured at about 60 µs a row with identical hits, so one shared rule for both verbs, independent of store size, is affordable once it lands, and the overlay's missing byte batches and deadline are hygiene owed today.

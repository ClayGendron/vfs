# Ten million files is the share estate of about 2,400 accounts in a million directories six deep with a 4 KB median file; measured enterprises change under one percent of their files on a working day and people run a fifth of a search a day while an agent session runs about forty tool calls; real search systems refresh in minutes and rebuild whole in weeks or never; and every file system at ten million files and beyond keeps directory aggregates as direct-children counters maintained with the write, labelled approximate, with recursive totals only where a directory is marked

On 2026-11-09 Clay proposed a reference scale for vfs, ten million files, a thousand users running a hundred queries a day, one percent of files edited during the work day and ten percent nightly in batch, six full reindexes a day and a partial reindex every fifteen minutes, and asked that it be written into AGENTS.md as a realistic enterprise scenario to filter ideas through. A first draft was written from assumption and Clay asked: "does this seam realistic for numbers? Maybe we can research what a realistic at scale scenario would look like before we make large assumptions?" The draft was withdrawn and this memo replaces it. Four researchers, fresh agents with no knowledge of the author's reasoning, each worked a 45-minute box on one dimension, the shape of the corpus, the workload, the indexing cadence, and how large file systems keep directory aggregates, and all four finished inside their box (in 16, 12, 12 and 13 minutes). While the studies ran, Clay added two statements that this memo records as the owner's decisions rather than as evidence: "queries per day is agent queries, not user queries", and "we need at least a daily re-index because of the design, but are going to create 15 min mini indexes that are cheaper but create multiple posting list tables". The brief, the four studies and their experiments are under [the studies folder](studies/2026-11-09-what-an-enterprise-scale-deployment-looks-like/). There is no intention: the memo exists to source the reference deployment in [AGENTS.md](../../AGENTS.md), "The reference deployment", and to rescale the aggregation experiment named in [the aggregation memo](2026-11-09-how-file-systems-and-search-tools-shape-aggregation-apis.md). This memo commits to nothing; the AGENTS.md table it proposes is Clay's to accept, and the specs that follow decide.

## 1. The answer in one paragraph

READ, across the four studies. Ten million files is what about 2,400 accounts hold on corporate shares at the measured rate of 4,225 files per account, or what a 1,000-person company holds once its code, wiki versions and cloud drives are counted, and it comes with about a million directories, a median directory of two files, a quarter of directories empty of files, a tree averaging six deep with 15% of directories at depth eight or more, a 4 KB median file that has not moved since 1981, a mean between 0.3 and 0.7 MB, and a count growing about a quarter a year. Measured enterprises change well under one percent of their files on a working day: at most 0.95% by the loosest reading of the desktop studies, a few tenths by the tighter ones, about 0.1% of stored bytes on shared servers, and one to five files a person a day by human saves and commits. People search their intranet a fifth of a time a day or less; an AI agent session runs about forty tool calls, so a hundred-plus calls a day is an agent's number, as Clay confirmed. Real search systems refresh in one second to fifteen minutes and rebuild their whole index weekly, monthly or never, and vfs's own posting build today rebuilds whole whenever anything is dirty. Every file system that runs at ten million files and beyond keeps directory aggregates as counters maintained with the write, direct-children everywhere and recursive only for marked directories, labels the counter approximate, bounds its lag in seconds, and keeps a full scan as the repair path. INFERRED: of Clay's seven proposed figures, four survive as stated (ten million files, the fifteen-minute partial pass, the ten percent nightly batch as a chosen contract, and a hundred calls a day once labelled as an agent's), two are relabelled (one percent a day becomes the ceiling over a half-percent centre; the thousand users become either 2,400 share accounts or a thousand people with everything counted), and one is replaced by the owner's own revision (six full rebuilds a day become one, required by the design, with fifteen-minute delta posting tables folded in by the daily rebuild).

## 2. The corpus: a million directories, six deep, a 4 KB median, a quarter a year of growth

READ, study 01. Varonis's 2019 assessment of 785 organizations' on-premises and hybrid shares counted 53.9 billion files, 4.33 billion folders and 12.75 million accounts, which is 4,225 files and 340 folders per account and 12.4 files per folder; its 2017 sample of 80 organizations, half with under 1,000 employees, had a median estate of 6 TB and 12.2 files per folder. The Microsoft desktop series measured 10 to 12.5 files per directory in 2000 to 2004 and 6.25 in 2009, a median directory of 2 files in every year, 23 to 25% of directories holding no files, mean depth rising from 6.1 to 6.9, and 15% of directories at depth 8 or more. The median file size is 4 KB in 1998, 2004 and 2009 and "remarkably consistent since at least 1981"; the mean is 318 KB on 2009 desktops and about 0.7 MB on 2018 shares. File and directory counts grew 20 to 32% a year on desktops over eleven years; a mature code tree grows about 5%.

EXECUTED, study 01. Seven sibling clones measured on 2026-11-09: linux has 93,878 tracked files in 6,161 directories (15.2 per directory, mean depth 3.81, largest directory 1,533 entries, median blob 3.8 KB), freebsd-src 114,621 files in 9,857 directories, gitlabhq 70,236 in 13,040, go 16,190 in 1,837, cpython 5,985 in 522, postgres 7,726 in 712, sqlite 2,218 in 54. Code trees are shallower (mean depth 2.5 to 4.5) and smaller-filed (median 1.3 to 4.3 KB) than desktops and shares, with the same 5 to 15 files per directory.

READ, study 01. The hosted services publish limits, not tenant sizes: a SharePoint library holds up to 30,000,000 items and a tenant's pool is 1 TB plus 10 GB per licence; a Google Drive folder holds 500,000 items and nests 100 deep; Confluence's Medium band is 500,000 to 2.5 million content versions for 1,000 to 10,000 users; the Windows repository is 3.5 million files for about 4,000 engineers, 875 files per engineer. Microsoft's platform adds "over 3 billion new documents" a workday across 400 million active users, 7.5 per user per day, a ceiling on growth that counts every object the platform mints.

INFERRED. Ten million files is the right round figure to keep and the table must say which company it describes: about 2,400 share accounts at the measured rate, or 1,000 people once a few hundred thousand code files, half a million to two and a half million wiki versions and their cloud drives are added to roughly four million share files. The million directories, the depth of six and the two-file median directory are the figures the proposal lacked, and they are what the aggregate question in section 5 is costed against: a write touches one parent among a million, a recursive counter touches six ancestors on average and twelve or more in the tail, and a `GROUP BY` at the root touches everything.

## 3. The workload: people barely write and barely search; agents issue a hundred-plus calls a day in a three-to-one read-to-write mix

READ, study 02. Meyer and Bolosky found about 20% of desktop files modified in the trailing month in 2009, which spread evenly over 21 working days is at most 0.95% of files a day and is an upper bound because many files are touched on several days; Agrawal's median file age of 80 to 160 days sits below it; Leung measured "somewhat more than 90% of the active storage on the file servers was untouched over the three month trace period" on two NetApp servers in 2007, about 0.1% of bytes a day. Veeam's sizing default of 2 to 5% a day is bytes on server fleets, inflated by databases and virtual disks, not files in a document corpus. Human writes are a handful a day: 0.64 human commits per Google developer per workday in 2015 with automation issuing 60% of all commits, 2.4 file saves per registered Dropbox user per day in 2017.

READ, study 02. Two intranet query logs put human enterprise search at 0.046 queries per employee per working day over 60,000 employees in 2002 (9% of staff searching in a week) and 0.13 to 0.19 over 75,000 in 2018; public web search is about 4 a day. Anthropic's Claude Code report measures about 10 actions per prompt and about 4 prompts per session, 2% of sessions over 100 actions a prompt, and 20 running hours a week for its users; SWE-agent finishes a coding task in a median of 12 steps and SWE-smith's 2025 models in 25 to 29. Leung's peak hour is 3.9 to 4.6 times the working-hour mean, fewer than 1% of clients made 12 to 22% of operations, and 94% of files were opened fewer than five times in a quarter.

EXECUTED, study 02. Ten actions a prompt times four prompts is about 40 tool calls a session. At 2, 5 and 10 sessions per user per working day that is 80, 200 and 400 calls a user a day; for 1,000 users, 2.8, 6.9 and 13.9 calls a second as an eight-hour mean and 11, 28 and 56 a second at a 4× peak.

INFERRED, and confirmed by Clay on 2026-11-09 as the intended reading. The hundred calls a day is an agent's number: 500 to 2,000 times what intranets measure for a person and within a factor of two of what a coding agent runs in two or three sessions. The table names the caller and keeps the number, with a band of 100 to 200 and 150 as the single figure, and gives it an agent's verb mix, about three reads, lists, globs and greps per write or edit with ranked search under a tenth of calls. The one percent of files a working day is a ceiling over every file-count measurement and under every byte-count sizing default; 0.5% is the central figure and 1% the design ceiling, and the gap between human saves (0.01 to 0.05% of the corpus) and either figure is machine writes, which is where agents live. The ten percent nightly has no measured counterpart in an organic corpus and is the ingest contract vfs already declares in AGENTS.md, so the table carries it labelled as a contract vfs chooses, with Google's 60% automated commits as the precedent that automation can outwrite people.

## 4. Indexing cadence: fifteen minutes is standard, six full rebuilds a day is unprecedented, and vfs's build today is whole-corpus whenever anything is dirty

READ, study 03. SharePoint Server's continuous crawl defaults to every 15 minutes with a floor of 1 minute and a 4-hour clean-up pass; Microsoft's Copilot connectors default to "Incremental crawl every 15 minutes; full crawl every week" on a grid whose floor is 15 minutes; Glean's connectors cluster incremental crawls at 10 minutes to 1 hour and full crawls at 7 to 30 days, with webhook sources running no full crawl at all; Coveo calls a weekly rescan "sufficient" and reserves a rebuild for configuration changes; the retired Workplace Search ran a full sync every 72 hours, the densest default found. Inside the engine Elasticsearch and OpenSearch refresh every second, Lucene compacts by tiered merge and never rebuilds, and a whole-index reindex exists only for a mapping change. Sourcegraph polls every 45 seconds to 8 hours and forces a full per-repository build every 150 delta builds as a compaction device, GitLab every 200 and calls it "a band-aid"; GitHub's Blackbird indexes on push and states a whole-corpus rebuild of 15.5 billion documents would take about 18 hours as a capacity figure, never a schedule. Every vendor's stated reason for a periodic full pass is to see deletions and permission changes a foreign source hides; Elasticsearch, Lucene and the vector stores, which own their documents, never schedule one, and vfs owns its rows.

READ, study 03, the vfs tree. `reindex` has three grains in one verb: the dirty assessment is "linear in the dirty set" and the embed step is incremental by construction with a model swap as the one full re-embed, but `build_epoch` "rebuilds whole" the gram and lexical posting set whenever any live entry awaits encoding, holding the whole posting set in memory for the run, with "gram-range partitioned passes" named as the future direction (`indexing.py` lines 366 to 399). Freshness between reindexes is served by the `NOT encoded` overlay scan for grep and the lexical leg, and the vector leg's staleness is `reindex`.

EXECUTED, study 03. On the proposed corpus and the proposed change rates, six full rebuilds visit 60,000,000 files a day, 54.5 times the day's organic writes; one rebuild is 9.1 times; a weekly rebuild amortizes to 1.3 times. Ninety-six 15-minute passes a day each see about 2,500 changed files in working hours at the 1% rate, 0.025% of the corpus, and the nightly batch is one pass of 1,000,000.

INFERRED, with Clay's revision of 2026-11-09 recorded. The fifteen-minute partial pass sits exactly on the industry default and survives. Six full rebuilds a day has no precedent in any system studied; Clay's revision is one full rebuild a day, required by the design, with each fifteen-minute pass writing its own posting table and the daily rebuild folding those delta tables back into one. That is the zoekt delta-shard shape with a daily compaction in place of Sourcegraph's count of 150 builds, and it has precedent on both halves. Two consequences bind the table: the daily rebuild is 10,000,000 file visits a day, about ten times the day's organic writes at the central change rate, which makes the reindex path the largest write load in the system by an order of magnitude; and the fifteen-minute passes are affordable only once the build visits the dirty slice rather than the corpus, because on today's code each pass that finds anything dirty is a whole-corpus posting build, 96 of them a day.

## 5. Directory aggregates at scale: maintained with the write, direct-children everywhere, recursive only where marked, always labelled

READ, study 04. All eight systems studied keep whatever aggregate they offer as a counter updated as a consequence of the write, and none recomputes a subtree on the hot path. HDFS increments every quota-bearing ancestor synchronously inside the mutation under its single global lock, and that lock serves 150,000 to 250,000 operations a second over 1.1 billion objects at LinkedIn; its on-demand `getContentSummary` was seen holding the lock over 20 seconds and now yields every 5,000 inodes for 500 microseconds. JuiceFS, the closest analogue because it is a metadata engine over SQLite, MySQL and Postgres, keeps one `dirStats` row per directory holding direct-children length, space and inode count, coalesces deltas in memory, flushes every second in transactions of at most 1,000 rows as `x = x + ?` increments, keeps recursive totals only where a quota marks the directory, labels its fast mode "can potentially produce inaccurate results", and ships `--strict` and `fsck --sync-dir-stat`. CephFS alone keeps a recursive `rstat` on every directory, and CephFS alone needed a scatter-lock protocol to do it, throttles propagation at 1 and 5 seconds, documents its quotas as imprecise by "tens of seconds", and carries mailing-list reports of 24-hour lag with a maintainer writing that the update trigger was never fully defined. btrfs's precise qgroups raised transaction commit time by 76% and the project recommends against them; its fix was an approximate mode at 1.25%. ZFS keeps dataset totals "generally accounted for within a few seconds" and no directory totals at all; GPFS prints an `in_doubt` column and repairs with an I/O-intensive `mmcheckquota` run "when the system load is light"; FSRM keeps recursive bytes only on quota folders, seeded by a scan; HDFS logs "BUG: Inconsistent storagespace" when a scan disagrees and rebuilds every quota counter from the tree at startup, 55 seconds single-threaded on a large production image.

INFERRED. Clay's reaction to the per-write design, that "aggregations on each write seem like a lot of extra overhead", is answered by the systems that run largest in two parts. The overhead they accept is one coalesced increment per touched directory per write or per flush, never one update per file per ancestor in separate statements, and at the reference workload that is on the order of the write count itself, 50,000 to 100,000 increments a working day and about 100,000 touched directories across the nightly million, against a `GROUP BY` of 10,000,000 rows on every root-level `tree` that asks for a total. The overhead they refuse is a recursive counter on every directory, because that is the CephFS and btrfs shape and both carry the heaviest documented costs. The consensus, read against vfs's three proposed layers, is that the rollup columns on a directory observation are the direct-children counters kept with the write, that a recursive total is kept only for marked directories such as mount roots, that any maintained number is labelled and has a strict read and a repair verb beside it, and that the on-demand subtree scan remains as the strict path and the maintenance path with a yield budget, never as the verb. This is a reading of prior art, not a decision; the aggregation experiment that follows measures the two shapes on vfs's own engines at the reference figures.

## 6. The proposed reference deployment and the multipliers it gives

INFERRED from the four studies, each row carrying its justification. Files: 10,000,000, the share estate of about 2,400 accounts at 4,225 files per account, or a 1,000-person company counted with its code, wiki versions and cloud drives (study 01). Directories: about 1,000,000 at 6.25 to 12.4 files per directory, a median directory of 2 files, a quarter empty of files, a tail into the thousands of children (study 01). Depth: mean 6, 15% of directories at depth 8 or more, a tail to 12 to 15 (study 01). File size: median 4 KB, mean 0.3 to 0.7 MB, so 3 to 7 TB of bodies and about 40 GB of text at the median; the text share by count is high and by bytes low, and is not measured anywhere public, so it is entered as a stated assumption (study 01). Growth: about 25% a year, doubling in three years (study 01). Derived rows: about 100,000,000 chunks at the ten-per-file ratio vfs's own embed docstring states, with their postings and embeddings (study 03, reading `embed.py`). Callers: 1,000 people and the agents acting for them; human ranked searches about 0.2 a person a day, 200 a day in all; agent verb calls 100 to 200 a user a day, 150 as the figure, 150,000 a day, about 5 a second over an eight-hour day and about 20 a second at a 4× peak, in a mix of about three reads, lists, globs and greps per write or edit with `glean` under a tenth; the top 1% of identities issue 10 to 20% of calls (study 02). Organic change: 0.5% of files a working day as the centre, 50,000 single-file writes, 1% as the design ceiling, 100,000; human saves are 1,000 to 5,000 of them and the rest are machine writes (study 02). Nightly batch: 10% of files, 1,000,000, in 10,000-file batches, 100 batches inside six hours, about 50 files a second sustained, the ingest contract vfs chooses rather than a measured rate (study 02 and AGENTS.md). Partial index pass: every 15 minutes with a floor of 1 minute, each pass over what changed since the last, about 1,250 to 2,500 files in working hours and the nightly million overnight, each writing its own posting table (study 03 and Clay's revision). Full rebuild: once a day, required by the design, folding the delta posting tables into one; 10,000,000 file visits a day, about ten times the organic writes; prior art runs it weekly to never (study 03 and Clay's revision). Directory aggregates: direct-children file count, subdirectory count and byte total per directory maintained with the write and coalesced per batch, recursive totals only for marked directories, labelled approximate with a strict read and a repair verb, a 1 to 5 second lag budget where asynchronous (study 04, as the prior-art consensus the experiment tests). Rights: every verb call judged; the number of groups and the share of files under row grants is not measured anywhere public and is entered as a stated assumption. Engines: Postgres as the common case, SQL Server and Oracle at customer sites, so the 1,000-element `IN` list and about 2,100 bind parameters are live floors (AGENTS.md).

INFERRED, the multipliers. Per verb call, about 150,000 a day: a read must cost in proportion to what it returns, never to the corpus, because one scan of 10,000,000 rows per call is 1.5 trillion row visits a day, and an agent is waiting on the latency. Per file write, about 1,050,000 a day at the centre and 1,100,000 at the ceiling: one bounded statement per write is about a million statements a day and is affordable if it is bounded and uncontended, while the same statement landing on one hot row is about 30 updates a second on that row at the daytime peak and about 50 a second sustained through the nightly batch, a serialization point. Per index visit, about 11,000,000 a day, the partial passes' million plus the daily rebuild's ten million: anything added to the rebuild path is paid ten times more often than anything added to the write path and seventy times more often than anything added to a verb call, which is the multiplier that most often turns a small idea into an unaffordable one. Per batch, 100 a night in six hours: 10,000 rows through chunked builders at the engine floor is already about ten statements per table touched, and a round trip per row adds 10,000. Per corpus, once a day, the rebuild: affordable only there, only bounded in memory and interruptible, never per call or per write.

## 7. What changed from the proposal, and what the aggregation experiment now measures

INFERRED. Against Clay's seven figures: ten million files stands, labelled as whose estate it is; a thousand users stands as a thousand people with agents, with the hundred queries relabelled as 100 to 200 agent verb calls a day and a separate 0.2 human ranked searches; one percent a day becomes the ceiling over a 0.5% centre; ten percent nightly stands as a chosen contract; the fifteen-minute partial pass stands, with the caveat that it is affordable only once the build is incremental; six full rebuilds a day becomes one, by Clay's own revision, with the design reason recorded. The figures the proposal lacked and the table now carries are the million directories and their depth, the 4 KB median, the growth rate, the peak factor, the verb mix, the concentration of load on a few identities, and the directory-aggregate line.

INFERRED. The aggregation experiment named in [the aggregation memo](2026-11-09-how-file-systems-and-search-tools-shape-aggregation-apis.md) is rescaled to this table. It builds the reference tree's shape, about one directory per ten files, two-file median directories with a long tail, depth six with a tail past twelve, and measures three shapes at the 10,000-file nightly batch and the single-file daytime write: the on-demand subtree `GROUP BY`, direct-children counters coalesced per batch, and recursive counters on every ancestor, recording statements issued, rows inserted, counter rows touched, and rows scanned by a root-level and a mid-tree rollup read, with wall clock as a fact per engine. Study 04 predicts the first is unaffordable per call at the root, the second costs about one row per touched directory, and the third concentrates every write on the root's row; the experiment confirms or refutes those shapes on vfs's own engines.

## What this memo does not settle

The share of an enterprise estate that is text or an office document, by count or by bytes, is measured nowhere public; the table carries it as a stated assumption until a tenant or share estate is measured, an experiment that needs a real customer's listing and not a public source. The number of groups and the share of files under row-level grants in a real deployment is likewise unmeasured and entered as an assumption. Agent sessions per user per day is not published; the 2 to 5 band is inferred from Anthropic's 20 running hours a week, and a measurement from a vfs deployment's own logs is the experiment that fixes it. The cost of vfs's own `reindex` at ten million files was not measured, because the research brief forbade executing anything in the checkout; a bench of `build_epoch` on a real engine at one and ten million files is the experiment that sizes the daily rebuild. The translation of in-memory NameNode increments and JuiceFS's batched row updates to vfs's row shape with its indexes and locks is what the rescaled aggregation experiment measures next. Coveo's pages were read only through a search engine's extracts after five direct fetches returned HTTP 503, and the Potvin and Levenberg figures through secondary copies after the ACM pages refused the session; both should be re-read against the live pages before a spec cites them.

## One line

Ten million files is 2,400 accounts' shares in a million directories six deep; people barely write and barely search while agents run a hundred-plus calls a day; search systems refresh in minutes and rebuild in weeks, so one daily rebuild with fifteen-minute delta tables is the defensible cadence; and at a billion files directory aggregates are direct-children counters kept with the write, labelled approximate, recursive only where marked.

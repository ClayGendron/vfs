# Measured enterprises change well under one percent of their files on a working day and run a fifth of a search per employee, so 100 calls a user a day is an agent's number, not a person's, and the nightly ten percent is a contract vfs chooses rather than a rate anyone measured

Researcher 02, box opened 2026-11-09 09:11 EST and the study was written and linted by 09:23 EST, inside the 45 minutes. The question was the workload: how often files change and how often people and agents query, converted into verb calls per user per day and per second at peak for a 1,000-user company, compared with Clay's proposal of 100 queries per user per day, 1% of files edited per working day and 10% nightly. Every claim below is labelled EXECUTED (a command run here, output in the experiments folder), READ (a page or file cited in Sources) or INFERRED (my arithmetic from the two). The arithmetic lives in [experiments/02-the-workload-change-rates-and-query-rates/workload_arithmetic.py](experiments/02-the-workload-change-rates-and-query-rates/workload_arithmetic.py) with its output beside it.

The short version. Three independent measurements put the organic daily change rate of enterprise files at a fraction of a percent of files by count (Microsoft desktops 2009: 20% of files modified in the trailing month; NetApp file servers 2007: over 90% of stored bytes untouched in three months; backup vendors: 2–5% of bytes a day, inflated by databases and virtual disks). Two intranet query logs put human enterprise search at 0.05 to 0.2 queries per employee per working day, and even public web search is about four a day. The only population that issues a hundred calls a day is an AI agent: Anthropic's Claude Code report measures about 10 actions per prompt and four prompts per session, so 100 calls a day is two or three agent sessions. The measured peak-to-mean factor on real traces is 1.6× to 4.6× the working-hour mean. Nothing measured supports a 10% nightly churn in an organic corpus; that figure is an ingest contract vfs chooses to support, and should be written as one.

## 1. The file-system studies: how much of a corpus changes

READ. Agrawal et al. (FAST 2007) took annual metadata snapshots from 63,398 Windows desktop file systems at Microsoft, 2000–2004. Section 3.3: "The median file age ranges between 80 and 160 days across datasets, with no clear trend over time." Section 1: "the median file system had 30% of its files created or modified locally, and four years later this percentage was 22%", the rest being installed, never-written files. This is a measurement of desktops, not of shared corpora; file age here is time since modification, so a median of 80–160 days says that half the files on a desktop had not been written in the last three to five months.

READ. Meyer and Bolosky (FAST 2011) scanned 857 Microsoft desktops weekly for four weeks (18 September to 16 October 2009). Section 4.4.2: "Most files are modified between one month and a year ago, but about 20% are modified within the last month." Section 3.4 says of the data itself that "the content of file systems does not change rapidly" and that the data set allowed only weekly, not daily, backup analysis. Measurement, desktops, 2009.

INFERRED. If the 20% of files modified in the trailing month were spread evenly over 21 working days, the daily share of distinct files modified is at most 0.95% of the corpus; it is an upper bound because many of those files are touched on several days (the backup forums below say the same of bytes). Agrawal's median age of 80–160 days is consistent: an even spread would need a few tenths of a percent a day.

READ. Leung et al. (USENIX 2008) traced CIFS on two NetApp file servers for 65 and 97 days in late 2007: a corporate server (3 TB used, "over 1000 marketing, sales, and finance employees", 5,261 client IPs, 228 million operations) and an engineering server (19 TB used, "over 500 engineering employees", 2,654 clients, 352 million operations). The paper's own words: "somewhat more than 90% of the active storage on the file servers was untouched over the three month trace period"; read-to-write byte ratio 2.1:1 and 2.0:1, I/O ratio 3.2:1 and 2.3:1 ("in contrast to past studies that found ratios of 4:1 or higher"); "Over 66% [of files] are re-opened once and 95% fewer than five times"; "Fewer than 1% of clients account for" a large fraction of activity, with 0.02% and 0.04% of clients making 11.9% and 22.5% of opens; 76.1% and 97.1% of files were opened by only one client, and sharing "is rarely concurrent and mostly read-only". Peak loads: "The peak one hour request total for corporate is 1.7 million and 2.1 million for engineering"; "The peak one day intervals are 9.4 million for corporate and 19.1 million for engineering." Measurement, one vendor's own headquarters, 2007.

EXECUTED. From Leung's table: 228 million operations over 65 days is 3.51 million a day, or 667 CIFS operations per client IP per day on the corporate server; 352 million over 97 days is 3.63 million a day, 1,367 per client on engineering. The peak day is 2.7× and 5.3× the mean day; the peak hour is 3.9× and 4.6× the mean hour of an eight-hour working day (output in `workload_arithmetic.out`).

INFERRED. Three facts carry into the reference table. First, less than 10% of stored bytes were read or written in a quarter, so the organic daily write footprint by bytes is on the order of 0.1% of the corpus. Second, the read side is only two to three times the write side at the protocol level, because client caches absorb the re-reads; a vfs that serves agents without a client cache will see a higher ratio. Third, the load is concentrated: a handful of clients make a fifth of the calls, which for vfs means the top few agent identities, not the average user, set the peak.

## 2. Backup and replication vendors: the daily change rate they size against

READ. Veeam's Best Practice Guide, "Repository storage": "Typical daily change rate is between 2% and 5% in a mid-size or enterprise environment. This can greatly vary among servers. Database servers have high change rates (up to 30-50% in worst case). If there is a high percentage of databases involved, calculate with higher values like (e.g. 10%)." A documented sizing default, measured in bytes of changed blocks per day across whole servers (operating systems, databases, logs, virtual disks), not in files of a document corpus.

READ. The NetBackup 9.0 planning guide hosted by Cohesity tells administrators to measure the rate from differential backup sizes and works its example at "a 5% daily change rate". Scality's repository-sizing article works its example at a 2% average daily change rate and calls 10% a high-change workload. Both are worked examples, not measurements. I found no Rubrik, Commvault, Druva or Zerto page that states a typical rate; Commvault's community thread estimates incremental size as the last full plus 20%, a heuristic.

INFERRED. The backup figures are the loosest ceiling in this study: 2–5% of bytes a day is a server fleet's number, dominated by database and log churn, and vfs's corpus (documents, code and records behind the row-level gate) is the part of that fleet the file-system studies measured at a fraction of a percent of files a day. Taken as bytes, 2–5% is consistent with a few large files being rewritten, not with 2–5% of ten million files changing. A table that cites "1% of files a day" is therefore above every file-count measurement and below every byte-count sizing default, which is a defensible ceiling but not a central estimate.

## 3. Version control and content platforms: edits per user per day

READ (secondary; the ACM page refused this session). Potvin and Levenberg's January 2015 statistics for Google's monorepo, as reported by seroundtable.com and acawiki.org: about 25,000 developers, 40,000 commits per workday of which 16,000 are by people and 24,000 by automated systems, and a repository serving roughly 500,000 queries per second on a workday with 800,000 at peak, most of the traffic from distributed build and test systems. Vendor-published measurement of one company, 2015.

EXECUTED. 16,000 human commits over 25,000 developers is 0.64 commits per developer per workday; 40,000 total is 1.6. The peak-to-mean query factor is 1.6×. Automation issued 60% of the commits, and build systems most of the reads.

READ. GitHub's Octoverse 2026 reports 34.3 billion commits across public and private repositories in the year and more than 225 million developers. EXECUTED: 0.42 commits per registered developer per day, a weak figure because most registered developers are inactive and because an agent-authored commit counts the same as a human one. READ. Dropbox's January 2017 fact sheet: "more than 1.2 billion files are saved to Dropbox every 24 hours" against "more than 500 million registered users"; EXECUTED: 2.4 file saves per registered user per day, again over a mostly inactive denominator.

INFERRED. The content-platform figures land where the file-system studies do: a person saves or commits a handful of files a day, roughly one to five, and automation can issue more changes than all the people together. For a 1,000-user company that is 1,000 to 5,000 human file writes a day, or 0.01–0.05% of a ten-million-file corpus. The gap between that and Clay's 1% (100,000 files a day) is machine writes: pipelines, syncs, generated artefacts and agents.

## 4. Enterprise search: queries per employee per day

READ. Stenmark (2005) analysed one week (21–27 October 2002) of a Swedish vehicle manufacturer's intranet search log: "approximately 60,000 employees in the company group", 743,826 indexed documents, 5,644 unique IP addresses after proxy removal, 26,205 logged activities, 19,433 real queries, 11,419 sessions at 2.29 activities per session, 69.2% single-term queries. His longitudinal paper puts the same engine at 3,003 activities a day in 2000, 3,743 in 2002 and 3,861 in 2004, for a group that "employs some +80,000 people", with sessions averaging four to five minutes and most sessions a single query. Measurement, one corporation, 2000–2004.

EXECUTED. 19,433 real queries in seven days is 2,776 a day; over 60,000 employees that is 0.046 queries per employee per day, and the 5,644 unique addresses say that 9% of employees searched at all in the week. Counted over the people who did search, it is about 0.5 queries a day.

READ. Martin White (CMSWire, 2018) reports "a global high-tech manufacturing company with around 75,000 employees" with "around 200,000 searches per month" from "50,000 unique users", "on average employees were undertaking a total of four searches a month", application-launch queries included. A practitioner's client figure, undated, 2018 report. EXECUTED: 4 a month is 0.19 queries per active user per working day; over all employees, 0.13.

READ. Lewis (2010) analysed 65,000 queries to the Oak Ridge National Laboratory intranet from 17 September to 31 December 2007; the thesis page gives no staff count. Fagin et al. (2003) studied IBM's intranet (about 20 million pages crawled, 4.6 million non-duplicate) but publish the query distribution, not the volume. READ. The McKinsey Global Institute's 2012 exhibit puts "searching and gathering information" at 19% of the knowledge worker's week, sourced to IDC; the IDC "2.5 hours a day" figure is a 2001 report I could not reach, so it stands as a vendor-cited estimate of time, not a count of queries.

READ. For an upper bound, SparkToro's 2024 Datos panel (about 130,000 US devices, 21 months, 331.7 million searches) measures about 121 Google searches per searcher per month, or about 200 with the long tail. EXECUTED: 4 web searches a day, every day, from a population that searches the web far more than it searches an intranet.

INFERRED. The two intranet measurements agree to within a factor of four: 0.05 to 0.2 queries per employee per working day, with a tenth of employees searching in any week and the searchers doing about half a query a day. The web upper bound is 4 a day. Clay's 100 queries per user per day is 500 to 2,000 times the measured intranet rate and 25 times the web rate; no human population searches that much. The 19% of time figure does not rescue it: time spent looking for information is mostly browsing, asking and reading, which in vfs are `ls`, `tree`, `stat` and `read`, not `glean`.

## 5. Agents: tool calls per task and per day

READ. Anthropic's Claude Code report (about 400,000 interactive sessions from about 235,000 people, October 2025 to April 2026): "In a typical session, there are about four such turns"; "each prompt the user sends sets off a chain of around 10 actions taken by Claude on average"; novice prompts about five actions and expert prompts about twelve; when Claude plans, "about 16"; "About 2% of sessions average more than 100 actions per prompt", 1 in 270 more than 200, 1 in 2,300 more than 500; "Claude Code users now spend an average of 20 hours per week using the tool", measured as hours the tool was running. Measurement of a hosted product's users, 2026. The report gives no sessions per user per day.

READ. Anthropic's internal study (200,000 Claude Code transcripts, February and August 2025, 132 engineers surveyed): the maximum run of consecutive tool calls without human input rose from 9.8 to 21.2; human turns per transcript fell from 6.2 to 4.1; merged pull requests per engineer per day rose 67%. Measurement of one company's engineers with frontier access. The June 2026 Economic Index report gives turns per conversation by occupation and hourly sampling, but no per-user daily counts.

READ. SWE-agent (arXiv 2405.15793, GPT-4 Turbo on SWE-bench): resolved runs "finish with a median cost of $1.21 and 12 steps compared to a mean of $2.52 and 21 steps for unsuccessful ones", with a $4 cap per instance; on the full test set "an average of 14.71 turns to finish a trajectory, with a median of 12 turns and 75% of trajectories being completed within 18 turns"; from turn five the dominant actions are edit and execute. SWE-smith reports 24.9 to 29.1 steps per SWE-bench Verified task for 2025 models. Benchmark measurements, one task each.

EXECUTED. Ten actions a prompt times four prompts is about 40 tool calls per Claude Code session, which matches a coding benchmark task of 12 to 29 steps run two or three times. At 2, 5 and 10 sessions per user per working day the verb-call load is 80, 200 and 400 calls per user per day; for 1,000 users that is 2.8, 6.9 and 13.9 calls a second as an eight-hour mean, and 11, 28 and 56 a second at a 4× peak.

INFERRED. Sessions per user per day is the one figure not published anywhere I reached. The proxy is Anthropic's 20 running hours a week, four hours a working day; at four to five minutes a turn and four turns a session, that is a wide range of 5 to 15 sessions a day for a heavy user, and the report's population is self-selected heavy users of a coding tool. For a whole company where agents are a share of the staff's work rather than all of it, 2 to 5 sessions a day per person, or 100 to 200 calls, is the honest band. Clay's 100 a day sits at the bottom of it. In verb terms the mix is a Claude Code mix: most calls are `read`, `grep`, `glob` and `ls`, edits are a quarter to a third (Leung's 2–3:1 at the protocol level is the same shape), and ranked search is a small fraction, because agents search the way coding agents do, by `grep` and path, with `glean` for the first orientation step.

## 6. Comparing the families

INFERRED. On change rate the four families disagree by two orders of magnitude and the disagreement is the unit. By distinct files on desktops: at most 0.95% a day (Meyer), more likely a few tenths (Agrawal's median age). By bytes on shared file servers: about 0.1% a day (Leung's 90% untouched in a quarter). By bytes on server fleets: 2–5% a day (Veeam), because databases and virtual disks churn. By human edits: one to five files a person a day (Google, Dropbox, GitHub). Clay's 1% of files per working day is above every measurement by file count and is reached only when machine writes are included; it is a ceiling, and a reasonable one to design against. Clay's 10% nightly has no measured counterpart in an organic corpus: no study saw a tenth of a corpus rewritten daily. It is the shape of a bulk ingest or a re-render, which AGENTS.md already declares as a supported contract ("batches of 10,000+ files in a single call"); the table should carry it as a chosen contract, with Google's 60% automated commits as the precedent that automation can outwrite people.

INFERRED. On query rate the families agree once the population is named. People: 0.05–0.2 intranet searches a day, 4 web searches a day, and a few hundred to a thousand file-protocol operations a day (Leung's 667 and 1,367 CIFS operations per client). Agents: 12–29 tool calls a task, about 40 a session, and 100–400 a day depending on how many sessions. The 100-a-day figure is wrong for people by three orders of magnitude and right for agents within a factor of two to four. The useful correction is not the number but its label and its verb mix.

INFERRED. On peak factor the measurements agree: 1.6× (Google's build systems, a flat machine load), 2.7–5.3× for the peak day and 3.9–4.6× for the peak hour over the working-hour mean (Leung's human-driven servers), 5× in Microsoft's SharePoint worked example. Human load peaks at 4×; agent load is flatter but is itself bursty per session (2% of sessions exceed 100 actions a prompt). A 4× peak over the eight-hour mean is defensible and conservative for a mixed population; it should not be applied to a 24-hour mean, which inflates it threefold.

## 7. Proposed figures for the reference table

- Human ranked-search queries (`glean` by a person): 0.2 per employee per working day, 200 a day for 1,000 users, 0.03 a second at peak. Justification: the two intranet logs (0.05 and 0.13–0.19), rounded to their upper end.
- Agent verb calls: 100–200 per user per working day as the band, 150 as the single figure, 150,000 a day for 1,000 users, 5 a second as an eight-hour mean and 20 a second at peak. Justification: 10 actions a prompt × 4 prompts a session × 2–5 sessions a day, from Anthropic's measured report; Clay's 100 is the bottom of the band.
- Verb mix: about three reads, lists, globs and greps for every write or edit; `glean` under a tenth of calls. Justification: Leung's 2–3:1 I/O ratio and the coding agents' edit-and-execute loops after turn five.
- Peak factor: 4× the eight-hour working-day mean. Justification: Leung's peak hour at 3.9–4.6× and SharePoint's 5×; Google's 1.6× is the machine-load floor.
- Organic change: 0.5% of files per working day as the central figure (50,000 files at ten million), 1% as the ceiling to design against. Justification: Meyer's 20% a month is 0.95% a day as an upper bound; Agrawal's median age and Leung's 90% untouched sit below it; human saves alone are 0.01–0.05%.
- Nightly batch: 10% of files (1,000,000 at ten million) kept, labelled as the ingest contract vfs supports rather than a measured rate. Justification: AGENTS.md already declares 10,000+-file batches; Google's automated commits (60% of all) show automation can dominate writes; no organic study measured anything near it.
- Concentration: the top 1% of identities issue 10–20% of calls, and 94% of files are touched fewer than five times a quarter; file sharing is rarely concurrent. Justification: Leung's observations 6, 8 and 10. The per-user average understates what the busiest agent identity does.
- Quarterly footprint: under 10% of stored bytes read or written. Justification: Leung, verbatim.

The one figure in Clay's proposal my evidence most changes is "100 queries per user per day". As a human search rate it is 500 to 2,000 times what intranets measure; as an agent's daily verb-call count it is right to within a factor of two, with ranked search a small slice of it. The table should name the caller and the verb, and keep the number.

## What this study does not settle

Sessions per user per day for agents is not published; the 2–5 band is inferred from Anthropic's 20 running hours a week and the report's turn counts. The Potvin and Levenberg figures were confirmed only through secondary reports because the ACM pages refused this session. No Rubrik, Commvault, Druva or Zerto document stating a default change rate was found inside the box. The ORNL and IBM logs give query counts without staff counts. No study measured an agent-driven file corpus directly; the agent figures are from coding tools over code repositories, and the file-system figures are from people over documents; the table joins them by assumption.

## One line

People barely search and barely write (a fifth of a query and a few saves a day, under one percent of files a day); agents do a hundred-plus calls a day in a three-to-one read-to-write mix; the nightly ten percent is a contract vfs chooses, not a rate anyone measured.

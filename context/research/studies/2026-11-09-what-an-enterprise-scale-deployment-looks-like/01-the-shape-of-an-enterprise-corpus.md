# Ten million files is what roughly 2,400 accounts hold on corporate shares, in about a million directories averaging six levels deep, with a 4 KB median file that has not moved since 1981 and a mean between 0.3 and 0.7 MB, growing 20 to 30 percent a year

The box opened at 09:11:58 EST on 2026-11-09 and this study was finished inside it. The question was the shape of an enterprise corpus: how many files a large enterprise holds, in how many directories, how deep, how big, and how fast it grows, measured rather than assumed. Four families of evidence answer it: the Microsoft desktop file-system studies of 1998, 2000 to 2004 and 2009 (READ); Varonis's risk-assessment reports, the only published measurement of whole corporate share estates I found, covering 80 organizations in 2016 and 785 in 2019 (READ); the documented limits and the few published scale figures of SharePoint, OneDrive, Google Drive, Dropbox, Box and Confluence (READ); and the large code repositories, both the published figures for Google, Windows and Meta (READ) and seven source trees measured directly in the sibling clones (EXECUTED). The headline: a 1,000-knowledge-worker company holds about 4 million files on shared storage by the measured per-account rate (4 to 9 million by the median organization's terabytes), a 10,000-worker company holds about 40 million, and 10 million files is the share estate of a company of about 2,400 accounts, or of a 1,000-person company once its code, wiki and cloud drives are counted in; it comes with 0.8 to 1.6 million directories (one million is the round figure), a mean depth of 6 to 7 with a tail past 12, a 4 KB median and a 0.3 to 0.7 MB mean file (3 to 7 TB of bodies), and a file count growing 20 to 32 percent a year on desktops, with code trees growing far slower at about 5 percent.

## 1. The Microsoft desktop studies: three snapshots of the same population eleven years apart

READ. Douceur and Bolosky scanned 10,568 file systems on 4,801 Windows desktops in 1998 and found 140,647,042 files in 11,055,804 MB (their Table 1), which is 13,300 files per file system and a mean file size of 78.6 KB (INFERRED from the table). The median file size is 4 KB, and on 50% of file systems the median lies between 2 and 8 KB. The median directory holds 2 files, 18% of directories hold no files, 69% hold no subdirectory, and fewer than 0.5% hold more than twenty subdirectories; both directory-size histograms fit an offset inverse-polynomial law, which is why the mean is five to six times the median. Depth fits a Poisson distribution with λ 4.38 for directories and 4.43 for files once two Windows artefacts (System32 at depth 2 holding 5.5% of files, the web cache at depth 5 holding 3.8%) are removed, and 15% of all directories sit at depth 8 or greater.

READ. Agrawal, Bolosky, Douceur and Lorch followed the same population from 2000 to 2004 across 63,398 file systems. Files per file system: mean 30K to 90K, median 18K to 52K. Directories per file system: mean 2,400 to 8,900, median 1K to 4K. Mean directory size fell from 12.5 to 10.2 files while the median stayed at 2; 23 to 25% of directories held no files in every year. Mean depth rose from 6.1 to 6.9, median from 5 to 6. Mean file size rose from 108 KB to 189 KB, which the authors estimate as "roughly 15% per year"; the byte-weighted median rose from 3 MB to 9 MB; 1 to 1.5% of files are empty. Consumed space per file system rose from an arithmetic mean of 3 GB to 18 GB. Eight extensions account for over 35% of files and DLLs hold more bytes than any other type in every year; the paper gives no share for text or documents. INFERRED: the file count grew about 32% a year compounded over the four intervals and consumed bytes about 43% a year.

READ. Meyer and Bolosky scanned 857 file systems on 597 machines in 2009, 59% Windows 7, 18% Server 2008. Mean capacity 194 GB, mean utilization 43% (53% in 2000). A mean of 225K files and 36K directories per file system, a mean of 6.25 files per directory ("even as users in 2009 have more files, they have fewer files per directory"), and a tree that is "deeper with a smaller branching factor"; bytes decline steeply more than five levels deep while files higher in the tree are larger than those deeper. Mean file size 318 KB, "about three times what it was in 2000"; the median "remains 4K (a result that has been remarkably consistent since at least 1981)". INFERRED from the 2004 and 2009 means: files grew 20% a year, directories 32% a year, mean file size 11% a year.

INFERRED. What these three studies measure is a knowledge worker's desktop including the operating system and installed programs, not the documents the worker authored, so the per-file-system counts (13K, 90K, 225K) are an upper bound on what one person brings to a shared corpus and most of that count is system files. What transfers to an enterprise corpus is the shape, which is remarkably stable across eleven years and three populations: a 4 KB median file, a mean 20 to 80 times the median and rising, a median directory of 2 files with a mean of 6 to 12, a depth of 6 to 7 on average with a Poisson tail, and a count of files and directories growing 20 to 32% a year.

## 2. Leung 2008 and Roselli 2000: the workload studies give storage per employee and little else about shape

INFERRED: about 3 GB of shared storage per corporate knowledge worker and 38 GB per engineer in 2007, before the 15% a year growth in mean file size and the move to cloud drives. READ. 75% of opened files were under 20 KB; 60% of bytes came from files under 10 MB; in engineering 12% of bytes came from files over 5 GB; C header files were 37.4% of engineering opens; "somewhat more than 90% of the active storage on the file servers was untouched over the three month trace period". The paper reports no total file count and no directory statistics.

READ. Roselli, Lorch and Anderson traced four environments of under 400 users in 1997 and 2000 and report access-size distributions (88% of accessed files under 16 KB in the instructional trace, 24% in the NT trace) but no static corpus shape, no file count per user and no directory figures. ## 6. Comparing the families: where they agree, where they differ and why

INFERRED. Files per directory agree across every family: a median of 2 on desktops (1998, 2000 to 2004), a mean of 10 to 12.5 on desktops and 12.2 to 12.4 on corporate shares, 6.25 on 2009 desktops, 5 to 15 in code trees; the mean is five to eight times the median because directory size follows an offset inverse-square law with a few directories of a thousand or more entries. The difference between 6.25 and 12 is population: the 2009 desktops carry Windows 7's deeper program trees, the shares carry user-organized folders.

INFERRED. Depth agrees in shape and differs in offset: desktops average 6 to 7 because the operating system's own tree (Users, AppData, Program Files) adds levels before the user's folders begin; code trees average 3 to 4.5; shares have no published depth figure and Google and SharePoint cap it at 100 levels and 400 characters respectively. A corpus mounted in vfs under a root like `/enterprise/<share>/` sits between the two, and 6 is the defensible mean with the Poisson tail that puts about 15% of directories at depth 8 or more and a few paths past 12.

INFERRED. File size median is the one invariant: 4 KB on Windows desktops in 1998, 2004 and 2009, "consistent since at least 1981", and 1.3 to 4.3 KB in code trees. The mean moves with the population and the decade: 79 KB (1998), 189 KB (2004), 318 KB (2009) on desktops, about 0.7 MB on corporate shares (2018), 2.5 MB per Dropbox item (2018); the gap is media, archives and virtual disks that hold most bytes and few files. For a text-indexing system the median governs the chunk and posting counts and the mean governs the body store.

INFERRED. Growth differs most by population: desktop file counts grew 32% a year (2000 to 2004) then 20% (2004 to 2009), directories 32% a year, mean file size 11 to 15% a year; corporate shares have no longitudinal measurement (the Varonis 2018-to-2019 jump from 6.2 to 53.8 billion files is sample growth, not estate growth); Microsoft's platform-wide 7.5 documents per active user per workday is a ceiling that would be 45% a year against a 4-million-file estate for 1,000 users; code trees grow about 5% a year. The share of files that is text or documents is not measured in any source read; the proxies are that 37% of engineering opens were C headers (Leung), that DLLs hold the most bytes on desktops (Agrawal), and that code trees are text by construction, which together say the text share by count is high and by bytes is low, without a number.

## 7. Proposed figures for the reference table

Files, 1,000 knowledge workers: 4 million on shared storage (4,225 files per account measured over 12.75 million accounts, Varonis 2019; 4.4 to 8.8 million for the median 6 TB organization of 2016, half of which had under 1,000 employees), plus 0.5 to 2.5 million wiki content versions (Confluence Medium band) and under a million code files (Windows rate), so 5 to 8 million in all; a 10-million-file reference is a company of about 2,400 share accounts, or of 1,000 people counted with their wiki, code and cloud drives, and is the right round figure to keep.

Files, 10,000 knowledge workers: 40 million on shares by the same rate, 2.5 to 10 million wiki versions (Confluence Large), a few million code files, so 40 to 60 million; the Varonis 2019 mean organization of 16,250 accounts held 68.6 million.

Directories per 10 million files: 1 million (12.4 files per folder on shares gives 0.81 million, 10.1 on 2004 desktops gives 0.99 million, 6.25 on 2009 desktops gives 1.6 million), with a median directory of 2 files, a quarter of directories empty of files, and a long tail whose largest directories hold thousands of children (1,533 in linux; SharePoint tolerates 30 million in a library and Google 500,000 in a folder).

Depth: mean 6, median 6, about 15% of directories at depth 8 or more, a tail to 12 to 15, and a hard ceiling set by the platform (100 levels at Google, 400 path characters at SharePoint); vfs adds its own mount prefix on top.

File size: median 4 KB, mean 0.3 MB (desktops) to 0.7 MB (shares), p99 in the low hundreds of KB for text and code, so 10 million files is 3 to 7 TB of bodies and 10 million files of text at the median is 40 GB.

Text or document share: not measured anywhere public; the honest table entry is a stated assumption, with the note that by count most files are small and text-like (the 4 KB median, the header-file-heavy engineering workload) and by bytes most of the corpus is media, archives and binaries that vfs stores but does not chunk.

Growth: 20 to 32% a year in file count and directory count on desktops over eleven years, 11 to 15% a year in mean file size, 5% a year in a mature code tree, and an upper ceiling of 45% a year from Microsoft 365's platform-wide document creation rate; 25% a year is the round figure, so a 10-million-file corpus is 12.5 million a year later and doubles in about three years.

Storage per knowledge worker: 3 GB of shared files per corporate employee and 38 GB per engineer in 2007 (Leung), 10 GB per licence added to the Microsoft 365 pool, 1 to 5 TB per Google Workspace user, 70 TB per Varonis 2019 organization; a 1,000-user company's shared estate is single-digit terabytes and a 10,000-user company's is tens.

The figure in Clay's proposal my evidence most changes is the pairing of ten million files with one thousand users: measured per-account rates put a 1,000-user company's shares at about 4 million files and the 10-million mark at about 2,400 accounts, so either the user count rises to 2,500 or the file count is understood to include code, wiki versions and cloud drives, and the table should say which. The second change is additive: the proposal named no directory count or depth, and ten million files comes with about a million directories, a median directory of two files and a tree six deep, which is what the aggregate-maintenance question in the fourth study is costed against.

## What this study does not settle

No source read gives the share of an enterprise estate that is text or an office document, by count or by bytes; the figure in the table has to be an assumption until a tenant or share estate is measured for it. No longitudinal measurement of a corporate share or cloud tenant exists in public; growth rests on the Microsoft desktop series (2000 to 2009) and a platform-wide ceiling. Shares have no published depth distribution; the depth figure borrows the desktop studies' shape. The Potvin and Levenberg paper was read through a verbatim excerpt because the ACM pages refused this session, and the Phoronix kernel statistics and the Confluence load-profile page at its original URL likewise; the Varonis 2018 report (130 organizations, 6.2 billion files) was not fetched. Chromium's and Meta's directory and file counts were not measured: the Chromium checkout is too large for the box and Meta publishes orders of magnitude only. The go clone's blob-size pass failed because the clone is partial and `git ls-tree -l` reached for blobs over a network this session could not use, so go has file and directory counts but no size figures. Per-user figures for Box, Dropbox and Google Drive business tenants are not public; the Dropbox S-1 and Box post give whole-service ratios that mix consumer and business accounts.

## One line

Ten million files is the share estate of about 2,400 accounts, not 1,000, and it comes with a million directories, a tree six deep, a 4 KB median file inside 3 to 7 TB of bodies, and a count that grows about a quarter a year.

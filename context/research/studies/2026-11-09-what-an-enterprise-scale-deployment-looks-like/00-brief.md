# The brief: what a realistic enterprise-scale vfs deployment looks like in measured numbers, so AGENTS.md can carry a reference deployment that every idea is costed against

Clay asked on 2026-11-09, after proposing a reference scale for vfs of ten million files, a thousand users running a hundred queries a day, one percent of files edited during the work day and ten percent nightly in batch, six full reindexes a day and a partial reindex every fifteen minutes, and after a first draft of that scenario was written into AGENTS.md from assumption rather than evidence and withdrawn: "does this seam realistic for numbers? Maybe we can research what a realistic at scale scenario would look like before we make large assumptions?" Four researchers, fresh agents with no knowledge of the author's reasoning, each received the brief below verbatim with their own question appended. The box is 45 minutes each. The memo this folder stands on is [what an enterprise-scale deployment looks like](../../2026-11-09-what-an-enterprise-scale-deployment-looks-like.md).

## The brief every researcher received

You are researching for the repository at `/Users/claygendron/Git/Repos/vfs`, a Python library with a Rust engine that gives AI agents and the people they work for one governed namespace over an enterprise's files, documents, code and records, stored in a relational database (Postgres as the common case, SQL Server and Oracle at customer sites, SQLite for development). Its verbs are Unix-shaped (`ls`, `tree`, `stat`, `read`, `glob`, `grep`, `glean` for ranked search, `graph` for typed edges, `write`, `edit`, `delete`), every call is judged against row-level rights, and the file bodies are chunked, indexed lexically and embedded for ranked search. The project wants to write one reference deployment into its contributor guide, `AGENTS.md`: a table of concrete figures for a large enterprise that has put its corpus behind vfs for its people and its agents, used as a filter that every design idea is costed against before it is proposed. The first draft of that table was written from assumption and withdrawn. Your job is to replace assumption with measurement for the dimension in your question: find what real systems and published studies say, cite each figure to its source, and say how far the figure can be trusted and what it depends on. You may read the vfs tree read-only (`AGENTS.md`, `src/vfs/sync.py`, `context/standards/roadmap.md`) for what the project already assumes. You decide nothing; the memo built on your study proposes the table and the spec that follows decides.

Your deliverable states, for each figure you find: the number, its source by URL with the date you read it or by clone path and commit, what population it was measured on (a company's desktops, a hosted service's tenants, a monorepo, a published trace), when it was measured, and whether it is a measurement, a vendor claim, a documented default or a limit. Where the published figures disagree, give the range and say why they differ. Where a figure is simply not public, say so and give the best proxy you found with its weakness named. End with the figures you would put in the reference table for your dimension, each with a one-line justification, and with the one figure in Clay's proposal that your evidence most changes.

## The questions

Researcher 01, the shape of an enterprise corpus. How many files does a large enterprise hold, in how many directories, how deep, how big, and how fast does it grow. Find the published file-system metadata studies (Agrawal, Bolosky, Douceur and Lorch's five-year study of Windows desktops at Microsoft, 2007; Meyer and Bolosky's deduplication study, 2011; Leung, Pasupathy, Goodson and Miller's measurement of enterprise network file system workloads, 2008; Roselli, Lorch and Anderson, 2000; any later study of corporate shares, SharePoint or Google Drive tenants) and record what they measured for files per user, files per directory, directory depth, file-size median and mean, the share of files that are text or documents, and growth per year. Then find the public figures of hosted enterprise content services and large repositories: Microsoft's published SharePoint and OneDrive scale and limits (items per library, the 30,000,000-item list limit, files per tenant), Box and Dropbox enterprise figures, Google Drive, Atlassian Confluence page counts for large tenants, and the published sizes of large code monorepos (Google's monorepo in Potvin and Levenberg 2016, Microsoft's Windows repository behind VFS for Git and Scalar, Chromium, the Linux kernel, Meta's monorepo) with their file counts and directory counts. Say what a company of 1,000 knowledge workers, and one of 10,000, plausibly holds, and give the directory count and depth that go with ten million files. Write to `01-the-shape-of-an-enterprise-corpus.md`, experiments under `experiments/01-the-shape-of-an-enterprise-corpus/`.

Researcher 02, the workload: how often files change and how often people and agents query. For change rates, find what the file-system studies above measured for the share of files modified per day or week and the age distribution of files, what backup and replication vendors publish or document as a typical daily change rate for enterprise data (the figures Veeam, Commvault, Rubrik, Cohesity, Zerto, Druva or Backblaze use in sizing guides), and what version-control and content platforms report about edits per user per day. For query rates, find what enterprise search vendors and studies report for searches per user per day (Microsoft Search and Viva usage reports, Glean, Coveo, Elastic Workplace Search, Algolia, the older Google Search Appliance and FAST figures, IDC and McKinsey knowledge-worker search time studies, academic enterprise-search query-log studies), and what is published about AI agents' tool-call behaviour: how many tool calls an agent turn or a coding task issues (SWE-bench and SWE-agent trajectory statistics, Anthropic's and OpenAI's published agent evaluations, Cursor or GitHub Copilot usage posts, the Model Context Protocol community's figures), and how many agent turns a user runs a day. Convert the figures you find into verb calls per user per day and per second at peak for a 1,000-user company, stating every assumption, and compare with Clay's proposal of 100 queries per user per day, 1% of files edited per working day and 10% nightly. Write to `02-the-workload-change-rates-and-query-rates.md`, experiments under `experiments/02-the-workload-change-rates-and-query-rates/`.

Researcher 03, indexing cadence: how often real search systems reindex, fully and incrementally. Find the documented defaults and the practice for Microsoft SharePoint and Microsoft Search (continuous crawl interval, incremental crawl schedules, when a full crawl is required and how often administrators run one), Elastic's connectors and Workplace Search (full sync against incremental sync schedules, the documented defaults), Glean and Coveo (crawl schedules, near-real-time update paths), Lucene, Elasticsearch and OpenSearch (the refresh interval, segment merges, when a reindex of the whole index is needed), Sourcegraph and zoekt (the `zoekt-indexserver` reindex interval and its reasons; the clone at `/Users/claygendron/Git/Repos/zoekt` is available read-only), code-search and documentation systems that index on push or on change, and vector-store and RAG pipelines (how often embeddings are recomputed, and what triggers a full re-embed). Read the vfs tree's own assumptions in `src/vfs/sync.py` and `context/standards/roadmap.md` read-only and say what the project already assumes about reindexing. Then say what a realistic cadence is for a ten-million-file corpus: how many full rebuilds a day real systems do (and whether six a day has any precedent), how often incremental passes run, what fraction of the corpus an incremental pass touches, and what a full rebuild costs in file visits relative to the day's organic writes. Write to `03-indexing-cadence-full-and-incremental.md`, experiments under `experiments/03-indexing-cadence-full-and-incremental/`.

Researcher 04, how large file systems keep per-directory aggregates at scale, and what they cost. The question behind the reference deployment is whether vfs should maintain per-directory counts and byte totals on every write or compute them on demand; find how the systems that run at ten million files and beyond answer it. Study CephFS recursive statistics (`rstats`: `rbytes`, `rfiles`, `rsubdirs`, `rctime`, how the MDS propagates them lazily up the tree and what the documentation says about their accuracy and cost), HDFS directory quotas and content summaries (the NameNode's per-directory namespace and space quota counters maintained on every mutation, `getContentSummary`'s scan, and the published NameNode scale figures), btrfs qgroups and ZFS dataset and project quotas (what is maintained, what a rescan costs, the known performance costs of qgroups), Lustre and GPFS or Spectrum Scale project quotas and the GPFS policy engine, NTFS and ReFS directory quotas, and JuiceFS directory statistics (the clone at `/Users/claygendron/Git/Repos/juicefs` is available read-only; its `docs/en/guide/dir-stats.md` and `pkg/meta/quota.go` describe a non-recursive counter per directory flushed every second with a fast mode labelled possibly inaccurate). For each, record what is maintained per directory, whether it is recursive or direct-children only, when it is updated (synchronously on the write, lazily, or by a background pass), how staleness or inaccuracy is exposed to the caller, what the documented cost or contention is, and the scale the system is documented to run at. End with what the systems that run largest agree on. Write to `04-how-large-file-systems-keep-directory-aggregates.md`, experiments under `experiments/04-how-large-file-systems-keep-directory-aggregates/`.

# The LLM wiki idea and how other tools check themselves

This is study 01 of two, written on 2026-11-15 to [the brief](00-brief.md). The brief asks two things. First: find the primary source of Andrej Karpathy's "LLM wiki" idea and report what it says the wiki is, which operations it names, and exactly what its lint operation looks for. Second: read, in official documentation, how tools that check their own integrity are shaped (what each checks, whether it repairs, the shape of one finding, how it bounds its work, and what its name promises), and say what the pattern suggests for one verb that covers bytes, structure and content references. The vfs tree stood at commit `08c372affd1730739c27aec84b8e94e0d6ef1e87` (`git rev-parse HEAD`); this study reads no vfs code and runs nothing in the tree. What was done: the primary source was found and downloaded whole, about thirty public pages of official documentation and source were downloaded and read, and the findings are set out below with the pattern they share.

EXECUTED means a command ran and the claim reports its output. READ means read on a page cited by URL, on the read date 2026-11-15. INFERRED means this study's reasoning, or the researcher's background knowledge where the text says so.

One caution on every READ quote. Each page was downloaded with `curl` and turned into plain text by a small script that strips HTML tags. Quotes are copied from that text. Where a quote ran over several lines in the page, the lines are joined with single spaces here. No quote was compared by eye against the rendered page. No request carried anything about this project.

## 1. The primary source: what the LLM wiki idea says

**1.1 Where it is.** READ. The primary source is a public gist by Karpathy, one file named `llm-wiki.md`, at <https://gist.github.com/karpathy/442a6bf555914893e9891c11519de94f> (read 2026-11-15). The gist page shows it was created on 2026-04-04. EXECUTED: the raw text was downloaded from <https://gist.githubusercontent.com/karpathy/442a6bf555914893e9891c11519de94f/raw> with HTTP status 200 and is 11,985 bytes. Every quote in this section is from that raw text. The file calls itself an "idea file": "This is an idea file, it is designed to be copy pasted to your own LLM Agent". Its last section says: "This document is intentionally abstract. It describes the idea, not a specific implementation."

**1.2 What the wiki is.** READ. The idea is set against retrieval at question time, where "the LLM is rediscovering knowledge from scratch on every question". Instead, "the LLM **incrementally builds and maintains a persistent wiki** — a structured, interlinked collection of markdown files that sits between you and the raw sources." The key sentence is: "**the wiki is a persistent, compounding artifact.** The cross-references are already there. The contradictions have already been flagged." The person does not write the wiki: "You never (or rarely) write the wiki yourself — the LLM writes and maintains all of it."

**1.3 Three layers.** READ. The gist names three layers. "**Raw sources** — your curated collection of source documents. ... These are immutable — the LLM reads from them but never modifies them. This is your source of truth." "**The wiki** — a directory of LLM-generated markdown files. ... The LLM owns this layer entirely. It creates pages, updates them when new sources arrive, maintains cross-references, and keeps everything consistent." "**The schema** — a document (e.g. CLAUDE.md for Claude Code or AGENTS.md for Codex) that tells the LLM how the wiki is structured, what the conventions are, and what workflows to follow when ingesting sources, answering questions, or maintaining the wiki."

**1.4 Three operations.** READ. The gist names exactly three operations: ingest, query and lint. Ingest: "You drop a new source into the raw collection and tell the LLM to process it. ... A single source might touch 10-15 wiki pages." Query: "You ask questions against the wiki. The LLM searches for relevant pages, reads them, and synthesizes an answer with citations." It adds that "**good answers can be filed back into the wiki as new pages.**"

**1.5 The lint operation, in full.** READ. This is the whole of what the primary source says about lint: "**Lint.** Periodically, ask the LLM to health-check the wiki. Look for: contradictions between pages, stale claims that newer sources have superseded, orphan pages with no inbound links, important concepts mentioned but lacking their own page, missing cross-references, data gaps that could be filled with a web search. The LLM is good at suggesting new questions to investigate and new sources to look for. This keeps the wiki healthy as it grows."

**1.6 What that names, counted.** READ. The lint looks for six things: (1) contradictions between pages; (2) stale claims that newer sources have superseded; (3) orphan pages with no inbound links; (4) important concepts mentioned but lacking their own page; (5) missing cross-references; (6) data gaps that could be filled with a web search. It also has one output that is not a defect: suggestions of "new questions to investigate and new sources to look for".

**1.7 Two supporting files.** READ. The gist names two files that help navigation. `index.md` is "a catalog of everything in the wiki — each page listed with a link, a one-line summary". `log.md` is "an append-only record of what happened and when — ingests, queries, lint passes." So a lint pass is itself a logged event. The gist also says where the idea stops scaling without tools: the index "works surprisingly well at moderate scale (~100 sources, ~hundreds of pages)", and "as the wiki grows you want proper search".

**1.8 Why the lint exists.** READ. The gist's own reason is maintenance cost: "The tedious part of maintaining a knowledge base is not the reading or the thinking — it's the bookkeeping. Updating cross-references, keeping summaries current, noting when new data contradicts old claims, maintaining consistency across dozens of pages. Humans abandon wikis because the maintenance burden grows faster than the value." Lint is the periodic pass that catches what ingest missed.

**1.9 What the primary source does not say.** READ and INFERRED. The lint paragraph says nothing about bytes, hashes, checksums or storage damage. It says nothing about repair as a separate step, about severity, about a finding format, or about who may run it. It is an instruction to a language model, run "periodically", and the model both finds and fixes in conversation. INFERRED: in the gist, "lint" means a health check of content and of the link graph. It does not mean an integrity check of the store. The store under the gist's wiki is "just a git repo of markdown files", so byte integrity is left to git.

**1.10 Which of the six need a model.** INFERRED. Three of the six checks can be computed from a link graph alone, with no model. Orphan pages (3) are pages with no inbound link. Concepts mentioned but lacking a page (4) are, in their mechanical form, links that point at a page that does not exist; the semantic form (a concept named in prose with no link) needs a model. Missing cross-references (5) are partly mechanical (page A links to B, B never links back) and partly semantic. The other three need a model or a person: contradictions (1), stale claims (2) and data gaps (6). This split matters for a verb that works from rows: it can do the mechanical half and hand its findings to the agent, which does the semantic half.

**1.11 An earlier post, secondary only.** READ, secondary. Several write-ups say the gist followed a shorter post by Karpathy on X titled "LLM Knowledge Bases", dated 2026-04-03 by those write-ups. This study did not reach that post; X pages were not fetched. One secondary summary, <https://academy.dair.ai/blog/llm-knowledge-bases-karpathy> (read 2026-11-15, by a third party, dated April 3, 2026), describes a phase "Lint and Maintain" in which "The LLM performs health checks on the wiki": "Scans for inconsistent data", "Imputes missing information via web search", "Finds connections between concepts that could become new articles", "Suggests further questions to explore". These are that author's words, not Karpathy's. They agree with the gist's lint paragraph.

## 2. How tools that check their own integrity are shaped

Each tool below answers the same five questions: what it checks; whether it reports or repairs, and how repair is asked for; the shape of one finding; how it bounds or paces its work; what the name promises.

### 2.1 fsck and e2fsck

Sources, all read 2026-11-15: <https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man8/fsck.8.html>, <https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man8/e2fsck.8.html>, and the header comments of the e2fsprogs source files <https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tytso/e2fsprogs/master/e2fsck/pass1.c>, `pass2.c`, `pass3.c`, `pass4.c`, `pass5.c` and `problem.c` in the same folder.

**What it checks.** READ. `fsck` is "check and repair a Linux filesystem"; it is a front end that runs a checker per filesystem type. `e2fsck` is the checker for ext2, ext3 and ext4. It works in five numbered passes, and each pass announces itself. The pass titles in `problem.c` are "Pass 1: Checking @is, @bs, and sizes", "Pass 2: Checking @d structure", "Pass 3: Checking @d connectivity", "Pass 4: Checking reference counts" and "Pass 5: Checking @g summary information". INFERRED from background knowledge: the `@` codes are abbreviations the program expands, so these print as inodes, blocks, directory and group. An inode is the record that describes one file.

**What each pass does.** READ. Pass 1 "iterates over all the inodes in the filesystems" and tests that "The mode field of the inode must be legal", "The size and block count fields of the inode are correct" and "A data block must not be used by another inode". It also gathers facts for later passes: "Pass 1 is designed to stash away enough information so that the other passes should not need to read in the inode information during the normal course of a filesystem check." Pass 2 "iterates through all active directory inodes" and tests each directory entry, for example "The inode number should refer to a in-use inode" and "The first entry should be '.', and its inode should be the inode of the directory." Pass 3 "assures that all directories are connected to the filesystem tree": for each directory "it attempts to trace up the filesystem tree ... until it reaches a directory which has been marked 'done'. If it can not do so, then the directory must be disconnected, and e2fsck will offer to reconnect it to /lost+found." It also finds loops: "if pass3 sees a directory twice, then it has detected a filesystem loop". Pass 4's file is titled "Check reference counts". Pass 5's file is titled "check block and inode bitmaps against on-disk bitmaps".

**Report or repair.** READ. Both, and the caller picks. With no option it is interactive: "the program will ask the user to fix each problem found in the file system." `-n` is report only: "Open the file system read-only, and assume an answer of `no' to all questions." `-y` is repair everything: "Assume an answer of `yes' to all questions". `-p` is the middle: "Automatically repair (\"preen\") the file system. This option will cause e2fsck to automatically fix any file system problems that can be safely fixed without human intervention. If e2fsck discovers a problem which may require the system administrator to take additional corrective action, e2fsck will print a description of the problem and then exit with the value 4 logically or'ed into the exit code." So every problem is classed in advance as safe to fix alone or not. In `problem.c` that class is a flag on the problem's table row, `PR_PREEN_OK`. There is also an undo file: `-z` writes "the old contents of the block to an undo file" before overwriting it.

**One finding.** READ. Each problem is one row in a table in `problem.c`: a code, a message template, a prompt and flags. Two examples: `PR_4_BAD_REF_COUNT`, message "@i %i ref count is %Il, @s %N.", prompt `PROMPT_FIX`, flag `PR_PREEN_OK`; and `PR_3_UNCONNECTED_DIR`, message "Unconnected @d @i %i (was in %q)", prompt `PROMPT_CONNECT`. The code's prefix is the pass number. The prompts are a fixed list of repair kinds, among them "Fix", "Clear" and "Connect to /lost+found". So a finding carries: which pass, which object (an inode number), what is wrong with the expected value beside the found one, and the one repair on offer. There is no severity field; the preen flag does that job. The run's result is an exit code that is a sum of bits: "0 - No errors", "1 - File system errors corrected", "4 - File system errors left uncorrected", "8 - Operational error", "32 - E2fsck canceled by user request".

**Bounding.** READ. It is a whole-store, offline check: "in general it is not safe to run e2fsck on mounted file systems", and results printed against a mounted filesystem "are not valid". It skips work when the filesystem is marked clean, and `-f` forces a check "even if the file system seems clean". Memory is the stated cost: one option's note says "a 40 TB file system with 2.8 billion inodes will consume an additional 5.7 GB memory". Progress is reported through `-C`. One check is left out for speed: `-D` "will detect directory entries with duplicate names in a single directory, which e2fsck normally does not enforce for performance reasons."

**The name.** READ and INFERRED. `fsck` is "check and repair". INFERRED: the name promises a structural check of the whole store, run when the store is not in use, that may change the store. It does not promise that file contents are right; ext4's passes check metadata, not the bytes of files.

### 2.2 git fsck

Source, read 2026-11-15: <https://git-scm.com/docs/git-fsck>.

**What it checks.** READ. "Verifies the connectivity and validity of the objects in the database." The discussion section says: "git-fsck tests SHA-1 and general object sanity, and it does full tracking of the resulting reachability and everything else." So it checks three things: each object's bytes against its hash, each object's format, and the graph of references between objects. A reference database check runs by default too ("check the references database consistency via git refs verify").

**Report or repair.** READ. Report only. "It prints out any corruption it finds (missing or bad objects)". Repair is left to the person and to another copy: "Any corrupt objects you will have to find in backups or other archives (i.e., you can just remove them and do an rsync with some other site in the hopes that somebody else has the object you have corrupted)." The one write it offers is a rescue, not a repair: `--lost-found` will "Write dangling objects into .git/lost-found/commit/ or .git/lost-found/other/".

**One finding.** READ. Graph findings are one line each: `unreachable <type> <object>`, `missing <type> <object>`, `dangling <type> <object>` and `hash mismatch <object>`. The page defines each. "missing": the object "is referred to but isn't present in the database." "dangling": the object "is present in the database but never directly used." "hash mismatch": "The database has an object whose hash doesn't match the object database value. This indicates a serious data integrity problem." Format findings carry a named message ID and a default severity, listed in a table of more than seventy entries, for example "badTree (ERROR) A tree cannot be parsed", "duplicateEntries (ERROR) A tree contains duplicate file entries", "zeroPaddedFilemode (WARN)", "badFilemode (INFO)", "nulInHeader (FATAL)". The message ID is printed with the finding: "fsck prefixes the error/warning with the message ID, e.g. \"missingEmail: invalid author/committer line - missing email\"".

**Severity is configurable per rule.** READ. "errors can be switched to warnings and vice versa by configuring the fsck.<msg-id> setting where the <msg-id> is the fsck message ID and the value is one of error, warn or ignore." Only FATAL ones cannot be changed. Known-bad objects can be listed in `fsck.skipList`, and the page prefers that over silencing a rule: "it is better to enumerate existing objects with problems with fsck.skipList, instead of listing the kind of breakages these problematic objects share to be ignored, as doing the latter will allow new instances of the same breakages go unnoticed." It adds: "corrupt objects cannot be skipped with this setting."

**Bounding.** READ. There is a cheaper mode that skips the byte layer: `--connectivity-only` will "Check only the connectivity of reachable objects ... This speeds up the operation by avoiding reading blobs entirely (though it does still check that referenced blobs exist). ... Corruption in blob objects will not be detected at all." Dangling-object reporting can be turned off with `--no-dangling` "to speed it up further". The caller can also give starting objects: "An object to treat as the head of an unreachability trace."

**The name.** INFERRED. Git borrowed `fsck` for a tool that never repairs. Here the name promises bytes, format and connectivity in one command, and a report.

### 2.3 ZFS scrub

Sources, read 2026-11-15: <https://openzfs.github.io/openzfs-docs/man/master/8/zpool-scrub.8.html> and <https://openzfs.github.io/openzfs-docs/man/master/8/zpool-status.8.html>.

**What it checks.** READ. "A normal scrub examines all data in the specified pools and verifies each block's checksum. A thorough scrub additionally decrypts and/or decompresses blocks as they are read." The purpose is "to discover silent errors due to hardware faults or disk failure." The thorough mode, `-t`, exists "so that it will catch the rare type of corruption where the checksum matches the data, but decryption and/or decompression fails."

**Report or repair.** READ. It repairs without being asked, but only where it holds a good copy: "For replicated (mirror, raidz, or draid) devices, ZFS automatically repairs any damage discovered during the scrub." Where there is no good copy the damage is reported, not repaired; the page speaks of "an unrepairable checksum error".

**One finding.** READ. Findings are not printed by the scrub. They are read from a second command: "The zpool status command reports the progress of the scrub and summarizes the results of the scrub upon completion." `zpool status -v` "Displays verbose data error information, printing out a complete list of all files containing data errors since the last complete pool scrub", and `-vv` "prints out the complete list of all corrupt records within each corrupt file." The summary is counters per device (the JSON example has `read_errors`, `write_errors`, `checksum_errors`) plus a list of damaged file names. So a finding is a file name, and beneath it a record. There is no rule name, because there is one rule: the checksum did not match.

**Bounding.** READ. This is the most developed pacing in the set. A scrub runs in the background on a live pool; `-w` waits for it. It can be paused and resumed: `-p` "Pause scrubbing ... Scrub pause state and progress are periodically synced to disk. ... Once resumed the scrub will pick up from the place where it was last checkpointed to disk." It can be stopped with `-s`. It can be narrowed three ways: `-e` will "Only scrub files with known data errors as reported by zpool status -v"; `-C` will "Continue scrub from last saved txg"; and `-S` and `-E` give "the date range for blocks created between these dates". Only one runs at a time: "Because scrubbing and resilvering are I/O-intensive operations, ZFS only allows one at a time." Progress is reported as scanned and issued bytes, a percentage and a time to go. It is meant to be scheduled: the page gives weekly and monthly timer units.

**The name.** INFERRED. "Scrub" promises a full pass over stored bytes against their checksums, in the background, on a live store, with repair from redundancy. It does not promise a structural check.

### 2.4 btrfs scrub and btrfs check

Sources, read 2026-11-15: <https://btrfs.readthedocs.io/en/latest/btrfs-scrub.html> and <https://btrfs.readthedocs.io/en/latest/btrfs-check.html>.

**Two tools, two layers, and the documentation says so.** READ. "Scrub is a validation pass over all filesystem data and metadata that detects data checksum errors, basic super block errors, basic metadata block header errors, and disk read errors." Then a note draws the line: "Scrub is not a filesystem checker (fsck, btrfs-check(8)). It can only detect filesystem damage using the checksum validation, and it can only repair filesystem damage by copying from other known good replicas. btrfs-check(8) performs more exhaustive checking". The checker's page says: "The structural integrity check verifies if internal filesystem objects or data structures satisfy the constraints, point to the right objects or are correctly connected together."

**Report or repair.** READ. Scrub repairs by default where there is a replica: "The automatic repairs of damaged copies are performed by default for block group profiles with redundancy. No-repair can be enabled by option -r." Check is the reverse: "By default, btrfs check will not modify the device but you can reaffirm that by the option --readonly", an option that "exists to calm potential panic when users are going to run the checker". Structural repair is opt-in and warned against: "Do not use --repair unless you are advised to do so by a developer or an experienced user". The page files `--repair` under a heading "DANGEROUS OPTIONS".

**One finding.** READ. Scrub reports statistics, not a list: `-B` will "print scrub statistics when finished", per device with `-d`. The exit status carries the verdict: "1 scrub couldn't be performed", "2 there is nothing to resume", "3 scrub found uncorrectable errors". This study did not read a sample of the statistics block.

**Bounding.** READ. Scrub runs in the background on a mounted filesystem. It can be cancelled and resumed: after `cancel`, "The progress is saved in the status file so btrfs scrub resume can continue from the last position." The status file "is updated every 5 seconds". It has a throughput limit: `--limit` will "set the scrub throughput limit for each device", in bytes per second. The page states the load plainly: "The estimated device bandwidth utilization is about 80% on an idle filesystem", and the cadence: "The recommended period is a month but it could be less." Check is bounded by memory instead: its `lowmem` mode addresses "the high memory consumption at the cost of increased IO". There is one honest gap in coverage: files with the no-checksum attribute are skipped, so "while metadata for these files continues to be validated and corrected by scrub, the actual file data is not."

**The name.** INFERRED. btrfs keeps two names for two promises. `scrub` is bytes against checksums, online, safe to repair. `check` is structure, offline by preference, dangerous to repair.

### 2.5 SQLite: integrity_check, quick_check and foreign_key_check

Source, read 2026-11-15: <https://www.sqlite.org/pragma.html>.

**What they check.** READ. `PRAGMA integrity_check` "does a low-level formatting and consistency check of the database" and looks for: "Table or index entries that are out of sequence", "Misformatted records", "Missing pages", "Missing or surplus index entries", "UNIQUE, CHECK, and NOT NULL constraint errors", "Integrity of the freelist", and "Sections of the database that are used more than once, or not at all". It does not cover references between tables: "PRAGMA integrity_check does not find FOREIGN KEY errors." That is a separate command. `PRAGMA foreign_key_check` "checks the database, or the table called \"table-name\", for foreign key constraints that are violated."

**Report or repair.** READ and INFERRED. Report only. The three sections describe no repair option. INFERRED from background knowledge: a damaged index is rebuilt with a different statement, and a damaged file is recovered with a separate tool.

**One finding.** READ. The two commands return findings in two shapes. `integrity_check` returns prose: "strings are returned (as multiple rows with a single column per row) which describe the problems", and when clean, "a single row with the value 'ok' is returned." `foreign_key_check` returns a structured row with a location: "There are four columns in each result row. The first column is the name of the table that contains the REFERENCES clause. The second column is the rowid of the row that contains the invalid REFERENCES clause ... The third column is the name of the table that is referred to. The fourth column is the index of the specific foreign key constraint that failed." Clean is the empty result.

**Bounding.** READ. Three bounds. A cap on findings: "Pragma integrity_check will return at most N errors before the analysis quits, with N defaulting to 100." A scope argument: "if the argument is TABLENAME, then checking is only performed for the the table named and its associated indexes. This is called a \"partial integrity check\"." The page states what the narrower scope cannot see: "Because only a subset of the database is checked, errors such as unused sections of the file or duplication use of the same section of the file by two or more tables cannot be detected." And a cheaper mode: `quick_check` "does not verify UNIQUE constraints and does not verify that index content matches table content", and "runs in O(N) time whereas PRAGMA integrity_check requires O(NlogN) time where N is the total number of rows in the database."

**The name.** INFERRED. "integrity_check" promises a report on the store's own structure. "foreign_key_check" promises a report on references between rows. SQLite keeps the two apart and names each for its layer.

### 2.6 Postgres amcheck

Sources, read 2026-11-15: <https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/amcheck.html> and <https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/app-pgamcheck.html>.

**What it checks.** READ. "The amcheck module provides functions that allow you to verify the logical consistency of the structure of relations." Index functions check invariants such as "all B-Tree pages have items in \"logical\" order". The table function `verify_heapam` "Checks a table, sequence, or materialized view for structural corruption, where pages in the relation contain data that is invalidly formatted, and for logical corruption, where pages are structurally valid but inconsistent with the rest of the database cluster." An option cross-checks two structures against each other: with `heapallindexed`, the function "verifies the presence of all heap tuples that should be found within the index". The page is clear that this is a different job from checksums: "amcheck can be effective at detecting various types of failure modes that data checksums will fail to catch", and "Relation pages which are correctly formatted, internally consistent, and correct relative to their own internal checksums may still contain logical corruption."

**Report or repair.** READ. Report only, and firmly so: "There is no general method of repairing problems that amcheck detects. An explanation for the root cause of an invariant violation should be sought." Even the obvious repair is doubted: "A REINDEX may not be effective in repairing corruption." It also states the limit of any check: "In general, amcheck can only prove the presence of corruption; it cannot prove its absence." And its standard for a finding: "No error concerning corruption raised by amcheck should ever be a false positive."

**One finding.** READ. Two shapes again. The index functions return nothing when clean and raise an error on the first violation. The table function returns rows: "verify_heapam checks a table and attempts to return a set of rows, one row per corruption detected." The columns are a location and a message: `blkno` "The number of the block containing the corrupt page", `offnum` "The OffsetNumber of the corrupt tuple", `attnum` "The attribute number of the corrupt column in the tuple, if the corruption is specific to a column and not the tuple as a whole", and `msg` "A message describing the problem detected."

**Bounding.** READ. The module gives the caller a cheap check and a thorough one, and tells the lock each takes. `bt_index_check` "acquires an AccessShareLock on the target index and the heap relation it belongs to. This lock mode is the same lock mode acquired on relations by simple SELECT statements." It is the one recommended for live systems: "When a routine, lightweight test for corruption is required in a live production environment, using bt_index_check often provides the best trade-off". `bt_index_parent_check` checks more and takes a `ShareLock`: "These locks prevent concurrent data modification from INSERT, UPDATE, and DELETE commands." The table function takes a block range, `startblock` and `endblock`, so a check can be cut into pieces, and `on_error_stop`, which stops "at the end of the first block in which any corruptions are found". The cross-check is bounded in memory by a setting and is probabilistic by design: "approximately 2 bytes of memory are needed per tuple" for "no more than a 2% probability of failure to detect an inconsistency", and "Any single absent or malformed tuple has a new opportunity to be detected with each new verification attempt." The command-line wrapper `pg_amcheck` adds selection of "which schemas, tables and indexes to check" and parallel connections through `--jobs`.

**Who may run it, and what a finding may reveal.** READ. This is the one page in the set that speaks to it: "Permission to execute amcheck functions may be granted to non-superusers, but before granting such permissions careful consideration should be given to data security and privacy concerns. Although the corruption reports generated by these functions do not focus on the contents of the corrupted data so much as on the structure of that data and the nature of the corruptions found, an attacker who gains permission to execute these functions, particularly if the attacker can also induce corruption, might be able to infer something of the data itself from such messages."

**The name.** INFERRED. "amcheck" is "access method check". The functions are named `check` and `verify`. Neither word promises repair, and the tool gives none.

### 2.7 MediaWiki's maintenance reports

Sources, read 2026-11-15: <https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Help:Special_pages>, <https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Help:Redirects>, <https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Manual:UpdateSpecialPages.php>, <https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Manual:$wgMiserMode>, <https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Manual:$wgQueryCacheLimit> and <https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Manual:$wgFixDoubleRedirects>.

**What they check.** READ. MediaWiki lists a group of report pages under the heading "Maintenance". Each is one named check of the content's link graph. A redirect is a page that only points at another page. The ones the brief names: "Special:LonelyPages – This lists pages with no links to them", also described as "This lists orphaned pages", with `Special:OrphanedPages` as an alias. "Special:WantedPages – List of non-existing pages with the most links to them, excluding pages which only have redirects linking to them." "Special:BrokenRedirects – This lists redirects that lead to non-existent pages." "Special:DoubleRedirects – This lists redirects that point to another redirect." Others in the same group: "Special:DeadendPages – This lists pages without outgoing links", "Special:UncategorizedPages", "Special:UnusedFiles – This lists files that are not used", "Special:UnusedTemplates", "Special:WantedFiles – This lists missing image files and other files", and one that works from content hashes: "Special:ListDuplicatedFiles – This is a list of files where the most recent version of the file is a duplicate of the most recent version of some other file."

**Report or repair.** READ. Report only. The fix is an edit by a person: for double redirects, "you could look out for double redirects and eliminate them by changing them to be one-step redirects instead." There is exactly one automatic repair, off by default, tied to the one event that causes the defect: `$wgFixDoubleRedirects`, "Fix double redirects after a page move", default value false. The page warns why it is off: it "Tends to conflict with page move vandalism, use only on a private wiki or a wiki where page moves are restricted to a trusted usergroup." The repair runs "with a job queue" under a named account, "Redirect fixer by default", so the fix is attributed.

**One finding.** READ and INFERRED. A finding is a row in a list: a page title, linked. WantedPages orders by the count of inbound links, so the list is ranked by how much a fix is worth. INFERRED: there is no severity and no rule code beyond the name of the report; the report's name is the rule.

**Bounding.** READ. The reports are too costly to compute on each view on a large wiki, so they are computed by a batch job and cached. `updateSpecialPages.php` is "a maintenance script to update the information on the special pages that cache results in the querycache table." It can refresh one report: `--only=page`, for example `--only=BrokenRedirects`. Miser mode makes the cache the only source: it includes "reading special pages marked as expensive from the cache instead of regenerating them every time they are requested", so they "will only be updated when explicitly made to update". The cache is capped: `$wgQueryCacheLimit` is the "Number of rows to cache in 'querycache' table when miser mode is on", default 1000. The help page admits the staleness in its own lists: "(note: updates may not be immediate)", and for two reports, "It's updated monthly".

**The name.** INFERRED. These are "reports" and "special pages", under "Maintenance". The names say what is listed (lonely, wanted, broken, double), never "error". A wanted page is not damage; it is work somebody might do. This is the nearest prior art to the gist's lint: its orphan pages are LonelyPages and its concepts lacking a page are WantedPages.

### 2.8 Markdown link checkers

Sources, read 2026-11-15: <https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tcort/markdown-link-check/master/README.md> and <https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lycheeverse/lychee/master/README.md>.

**What they check.** READ. markdown-link-check extracts links from Markdown and checks "if they're alive or dead". lychee is "a fast, asynchronous link checker which detects broken URLs and mail addresses in local files and websites." lychee can also check the part of a link after `#`, which names a section inside the target: `--include-fragments` will "Enable the checking of fragments in links."

**Report or repair.** READ. Report only. lychee's nearest thing to repair is a suggestion, not an edit: `--suggest` will "Suggest link replacements for broken links, using a web archive."

**One finding.** READ. markdown-link-check gives each link one of three states: `status` is "a string set to either `alive`, `ignored` or `dead`", with the HTTP `statusCode` beside it. Its default report is one line per link with a mark, then a count: "3 links checked." and "ERROR: 1 dead links found!". Both tools also emit machine formats: markdown-link-check has a JUnit reporter; lychee's `--format` takes "compact, detailed, json, junit, markdown". lychee's exit codes separate "found something" from "could not run": "0 Success", "1 Missing inputs or any unexpected runtime failures or configuration errors", "2 Link check failures. At least one non-excluded link failed the check", "3 Encountered errors in the config file."

**Bounding.** READ. A link check is network-bound, so the bounds are about requests. lychee has `--max-concurrency` ("Maximum number of concurrent network requests", default 128), a timeout, retries, and a result cache on disk: "If the `--cache` flag is set, lychee will cache responses in a file called `.lycheecache` ... This can greatly speed up future runs", with `--max-cache-age` to expire entries. It has `--offline`: "Only check local files and block network requests", which is the cheap mode. Both have exclusions: `ignorePatterns` in one, `--exclude` and `--exclude-path` in the other. lychee also has a dry run that lists what would be checked: `--dump` will "dump all the links extracted from inputs that would be checked".

**The name.** INFERRED. "Link check" promises one rule about one kind of reference, and a report.

### 2.9 Linters whose finding format is copied: ESLint and Ruff

Sources, read 2026-11-15: <https://eslint.org/docs/latest/use/configure/rules>, <https://eslint.org/docs/latest/use/command-line-interface>, <https://eslint.org/docs/latest/use/formatters/>, <https://docs.astral.sh/ruff/linter/> and <https://docs.astral.sh/ruff/settings/>.

**What they check.** INFERRED from background knowledge, since the pages read assume it: a linter reads source files and reports places that break a named rule. It checks content against rules, not storage.

**Severity is set by the user per rule.** READ. ESLint has three levels: "\"off\" or 0 - turn the rule off", "\"warn\" or 1 - turn the rule on as a warning (doesn't affect exit code)", "\"error\" or 2 - turn the rule on as an error (exit code is 1 when triggered)". The warn level has a stated use: "when introducing a new rule that will eventually be set to \"error\"".

**Report or repair, and how repair is asked for.** READ. Report by default; repair by flag. ESLint: `--fix` "instructs ESLint to try to fix as many issues as possible. The fixes are made to the actual files themselves and only the remaining unfixed issues are output." There is a preview: `--fix-dry-run` "has the same effect as --fix with the difference that the fixes are not saved to the file system." Fixes can be chosen by kind with `--fix-type`: "problem", "suggestion", "layout", "directive". Ruff: "To enable fixes, pass the --fix flag to ruff check". Ruff adds the split that matters most for a store: "Ruff labels fixes as \"safe\" and \"unsafe\". The meaning and intent of your code will be retained when applying safe fixes, but the meaning could change when applying unsafe fixes." And: "Ruff only applies safe fixes by default. To apply unsafe fixes as well, enable unsafe-fixes in your configuration file or pass the --unsafe-fixes flag alongside --fix". The safety class is a property of the rule that an operator may move: "The safety of fixes can be adjusted per rule using the lint.extend-safe-fixes and lint.extend-unsafe-fixes settings."

**One finding.** READ. ESLint's JSON formatter example shows the record. Per file: `filePath` and a list `messages`. Per message: `ruleId` ("no-unused-vars"), `severity` (2), `message` ("'addOne' is defined but never used."), a location as `line`, `column`, `endLine`, `endColumn`, a `messageId`, and `suggestions`, each with a `desc` ("Remove unused variable 'addOne'.") and a `fix` holding a `range` and replacement `text`. So one finding is: rule, severity, human message, exact location, and zero or more proposed fixes as data the caller can apply. Ruff's JSON does the same and adds the safety class: "All fixes will always be displayed by Ruff when using the json output format. The safety of each fix is available under the applicability field." Ruff offers the same findings in nine formats, among them "json", "junit", "github" and "gitlab".

**Exit codes.** READ. ESLint: "0: Linting was successful and there are no linting errors", "1: Linting was successful and there is at least one linting error", "2: Linting was unsuccessful due to a configuration problem or an internal error." Ruff uses the same three and says so: "This convention mirrors that of tools like ESLint, Prettier, and RuboCop."

**Bounding.** READ. The scope is the paths given. ESLint's `--cache` will "Store the info about processed files in order to only operate on the changed ones." A threshold bounds how much is tolerated: `--max-warnings` is the "Number of warnings to trigger nonzero exit code". Known findings are silenced in place, with a reason, by a comment; Ruff: "To ignore an individual violation, add # noqa: {code} to the end of the line".

**The name.** INFERRED. "Lint" promises four things by now: named rules that can be turned on and off, findings with a rule code and an exact location, no change to anything unless a fix flag is given, and an exit code that tells clean from dirty from broken. It carries no promise about stored bytes.

## 3. The pattern across the tools

**3.1 The table.** READ for each cell, from the sections above; the layer column is INFERRED.

| Tool | Layer | Default | How repair is asked for | One finding | How the work is bounded |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| e2fsck | structure | asks per problem | `-p` safe only, `-y` all, `-n` none | problem code, object number, found and expected, one prompt | offline; skips a clean store; progress; memory is the cost |
| git fsck | bytes, format, graph | report | none; restore from another copy | message ID and severity, or `missing`, `dangling`, `hash mismatch` with an object | `--connectivity-only` skips bytes; start objects |
| ZFS scrub | bytes | repair where a good copy exists | automatic | a file name, then a record; counters per device | background; pause, resume from a saved point; date range; errors only |
| btrfs scrub | bytes | repair where a replica exists | automatic; `-r` turns it off | statistics and an exit code | background; cancel and resume; bytes-per-second limit |
| btrfs check | structure | report | `--repair`, filed as dangerous | not read | offline; low-memory mode |
| SQLite integrity_check | structure | report | none | a prose string, or `ok` | at most N findings, default 100; one table; `quick_check` |
| SQLite foreign_key_check | references | report | none | table, rowid, referred table, constraint | one table |
| Postgres amcheck | structure, cross-structure | report | none; "no general method" | block, offset, attribute, message | block range; light or heavy lock; stop on first; memory-bound sampling |
| MediaWiki reports | content references | report | a person's edit; one opt-in job | a page title in a named, ranked list | batch job; cached; capped at 1000 rows; one report at a time |
| Link checkers | content references | report | none; a suggestion at most | link, state, status code | concurrency cap; cache; offline mode; exclusions |
| ESLint, Ruff | content rules | report | `--fix`; `--unsafe-fixes` for the rest | rule, severity, message, location, fixes with a safety class | paths; cache of unchanged files; warning threshold |

**3.2 The names sort by layer.** INFERRED. "Scrub" is always bytes against checksums: ZFS and btrfs both use it for exactly that and nothing else, and btrfs writes "Scrub is not a filesystem checker". "fsck" and "check" are structure: whether records point at the right records and are connected. "Lint", "report" and "link check" are content: rules about what files say and which other files they name. Only one tool in the set covers all three layers under one name, `git fsck`, and it does so by offering a mode that drops the byte layer.

**3.3 Finding is the default, and repair is a separate ask.** INFERRED from the table. Eight of the eleven rows report by default, and a ninth, e2fsck, asks before each change. The two that repair unasked are the scrubs, and both repair only by copying a block that has itself passed its checksum. That is the one case where the right answer is known with certainty. Everywhere else repair needs a flag, and the flag comes in grades: e2fsck's `-p` against `-y`, Ruff's `--fix` against `--unsafe-fixes`, btrfs's `--repair` under "DANGEROUS OPTIONS". Two tools refuse to repair at all and say why: git ("find in backups") and amcheck ("An explanation for the root cause ... should be sought").

**3.4 A finding has the same five parts wherever it is structured.** INFERRED. Which rule (a code or message ID); how bad (a severity, or a safe-to-fix flag); where (an object, a row, a block, a file and line); what is wrong in words, with the expected value beside the found one where there is one; and what fix is on offer, as data. Tools that return prose instead (SQLite's `integrity_check`) are the ones whose output a program cannot act on. Tools that return rows (`foreign_key_check`, `verify_heapam`, ESLint's JSON) are the ones other tools build on.

**3.5 The run's outcome is separate from its findings.** INFERRED. Every tool with an exit code keeps three outcomes apart: clean; findings; the check itself could not run. e2fsck adds a fourth, findings that were corrected. A check that failed to run must never read as clean.

**3.6 A rule's severity belongs to the operator, within limits.** INFERRED. git, ESLint and Ruff all let a rule be set to error, warn or off. git keeps a FATAL class that cannot be lowered, and will not let a corrupt object be skipped. Known exceptions are listed by object (git's skip list, a `noqa` comment), and git says to prefer listing objects over silencing a rule, so that new cases of an old problem still show.

**3.7 Every tool that runs on a large store has at least one of six bounds.** INFERRED. A scope argument (a table, a path, a device). A range that can be cut and resumed (amcheck's block range, ZFS's saved point, btrfs's status file). A cap on findings (SQLite's N). A cheaper mode that drops the costly layer (`quick_check`, `--connectivity-only`, `bt_index_check`, `--offline`). A rate limit (btrfs's bytes per second). A cached result that a batch job refreshes (MediaWiki, lychee, ESLint). The byte-layer tools pair background running with pause and resume; the structure tools pair a scope with a statement of what the narrower scope cannot see.

**3.8 Each tool says what it cannot see.** READ, restated. SQLite: a partial check cannot detect space used twice. git: with connectivity only, "Corruption in blob objects will not be detected at all." btrfs: data of no-checksum files is not validated. amcheck: it "can only prove the presence of corruption; it cannot prove its absence." INFERRED: a clean result is always "clean under these rules, over this scope".

## 4. What the pattern suggests for one verb over bytes, structure and content references

Everything in this section is INFERRED. It is this study's reading of sections 1 to 3, offered to the memo, which decides.

**4.1 The named idea is the content layer, and only that.** Karpathy's lint is a health check of a wiki's pages and links. Three of its six checks have a mechanical form or a mechanical half: orphan pages, links to pages that do not exist, and missing back-links. The other three need a model. MediaWiki's reports add a fourth mechanical check that the gist does not name: two files with the same content. Its nearest prior art is MediaWiki's maintenance reports, not `fsck`. The gist never asks whether the bytes are right. So the idea the developer named maps onto the third layer of the brief's three, and the first two layers are an extension of the name beyond its source.

**4.2 One verb can still cover three layers, on git's model.** `git fsck` is the precedent: bytes, format and graph under one name, every finding carrying a rule ID, and a flag that leaves the byte layer out. The shape that follows is a verb whose rules are grouped by layer, where each layer can be selected or left out, and where each finding says which rule fired. e2fsck's passes add an ordering: check the records first, then the tree, then the counts, because each later pass relies on what the earlier one established. For three layers that reads: bytes and rows, then structure, then references.

**4.3 What each name would promise.** "Lint" promises a report, named rules, findings with a location, nothing changed unless asked, and severity an operator can tune. That fits the content layer exactly and the structure layer well. It fits the byte layer least: no tool in the set calls a checksum pass a lint, and a reader who knows the word will not expect it to read every stored byte. "Scrub" promises exactly the byte pass: slow, background, resumable, over everything. "Check" or "fsck" promises structure. Two shapes are consistent with the prior art. One: `lint` is the umbrella, with the byte layer as an opt-in thorough mode, as ZFS has `-t` and git has the reverse flag. Two: `scrub` stays the name of the byte pass and `lint` covers structure and references, as btrfs splits `scrub` from `check`. The evidence leans to the second for cost reasons: the byte pass is the only one that must read every body, it is the only one whose prior art is always paced, paused and resumed, and it is the only one where automatic repair has precedent. The first shape is right only if the byte layer is off by default.

**4.4 One finding record for all three layers.** The copied shape is: rule code, layer, severity, location (a path, and where it applies a row or version identity), a message with found and expected values, and a proposed fix with a safety class. A rule code per check lets an operator silence, promote or count a rule, as git's message IDs and Ruff's codes do. Findings as rows, never as prose, is what lets an agent act on them.

**4.5 Finding and repairing are two acts.** The verb reports. Repair is asked for separately, per rule, and in grades. The grade follows one question: is the right answer known with certainty. Where a verified good copy exists (ZFS's replica), the repair is safe and may even be automatic. Where a value can be recomputed from other rows (a count, a derived path, an index entry), the repair is safe in Ruff's sense. Where the tool must guess (a damaged body with no other copy, an orphan whose parent is gone), the prior art either moves the thing somewhere visible without destroying it (`lost+found`) or declines and tells the person (git, amcheck). A content-layer finding such as an orphan or a wanted page is not damage at all, and its "repair" is an edit by the agent or a person, as on MediaWiki.

**4.6 On a damaged body in particular.** The scrubs show what a store does when a checksum fails and there is no good copy: it names the file in a list and leaves the decision to the operator. None of the pages read describes a tool that makes a damaged object permanently unusable as its response; git's advice is to remove the bad object and fetch a good one. That supports a design in which a hash mismatch is a finding with a named repair path, and not a state that locks the file.

**4.7 Pacing must be designed in, not added.** At ten million files the six bounds of 3.7 are the menu. A scope argument (a subtree) with an honest note of what a subtree check cannot see. A keyed cursor so a run can stop and resume. A cap on findings returned. A cheap default that reads rows only, with the byte layer opt-in. A cached report refreshed by the batch pass that already visits every file, as MediaWiki's reports ride a maintenance job. And a logged record of each pass, which the gist itself asks for in `log.md`.

**4.8 Who may run it is a real question, and one page answers it.** amcheck's warning applies to any store with row-level rights: a finding names a location, and a caller who can run the check, and can cause damage, may learn about data it cannot read. MediaWiki's reports are lists of page titles open to every reader, which works only because titles there are public. The pattern suggests findings be filtered by what the caller may see, or the verb be limited to callers who may see everything in the scope they name.

**4.9 What the gist adds that the storage tools lack.** Two things. A lint's output includes suggestions for new work ("new questions to investigate"), not only defects, so severity needs a level below warning: MediaWiki's "wanted". And the lint is run by the agent, periodically, as part of keeping the corpus useful, so its findings should be shaped for an agent to read and act on in the same session.

## One line

Karpathy's lint is a periodic, model-run health check of a wiki's content and links (contradictions, stale claims, orphans, missing pages, missing cross-references, data gaps) and says nothing about bytes; the storage tools split by name into scrub for bytes, check for structure and reports or lint for content, nearly all report by default with repair as a separate graded ask, and a structured finding is always rule, severity, location, message and an optional fix.

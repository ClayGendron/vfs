## The brief

You are one of three researchers. The repository is vfs at `/Users/claygendron/Git/Repos/vfs`. Read `AGENTS.md` and `context/research/README.md` first; they bind you. Your study number is in the message that sent you here. Do only your own study and write only your own file.

**The question the three studies answer together.** vfs deletes by tombstone: `delete` marks a row deleted and keeps it at its path, `restore` brings it back, and `sweep` destroys for good. The developer has ruled that a move keeps the moved file's identity, as POSIX rename does, including when it replaces an existing file. Today the schema allows one row per path, tombstones included, so a replaced file has nowhere to go. The developer wants to know whether tombstones are designed correctly today, and what a better design looks like. The memo that stands on these studies will tell the developer, in plain words: what the design does today, where it is wrong or surprising, what other systems do, and which redesign options exist with their cost.

**The properties a good design would have.** Judge everything against these. A delete can be undone. An undone delete brings back the same file: its id, history, edges, owner and rights. A path freed by a delete can be used again, and what a new file at that path inherits from the deleted one is a deliberate choice. A move keeps the moved file's id, at any size, and a replacing move leaves the replaced file undoable. The irreversible verb is named so nobody runs it by mistake. Every verb stays bounded at the reference deployment in `AGENTS.md`: 10,000,000 files, 10,000-file batches, about 150,000 verb calls a day. Rights are never widened: a caller who could not read a file cannot read it, its history or its edges through a delete, a restore, a revive or a move.

**Label every claim.** EXECUTED means a command ran and you report its output. READ means read in a file you cite by path and line, in a clone by path and commit, or on a page by URL and read date. INFERRED means your reasoning.

- Delete a file, and a directory tree. What each read verb (`read`, `stat`, `ls`, `tree`, `glob`, `grep`, `search`) then returns at the path, by the system actor, a named caller and anonymous, and what the trash view shows.
- Restore: of a file, of a tree, of one row inside a deleted tree, and onto a path that has been occupied since.
- Revive: delete a file, then `write` a new one at the same path, as the same caller and as a different caller. Which id, owner, history, edges, grants and label does the new file carry. Then restore. Say plainly whether a second caller can come to hold or read anything of the first caller's deleted file this way.
- `mkdir` at a deleted directory's path, and a write beneath a deleted directory.
- Move: of a file and of a directory tree. Does the id stay. How many rows and statements a move of a 1,000-row tree rewrites (count statements with a `before_cursor_execute` listener). Move onto a live occupant, and onto a tombstoned one.
- Sweep: at the trash view and at a live path. Say exactly what `sweep` of a live directory destroys and whether anything warns first.
- Who may delete, restore and sweep, by rung, read from the gate.
- What the indexes and unique constraints on the entry table say about tombstones, and what a listing of a directory holding many tombstones costs in rows visited.

Then judge today's design against the properties above, one by one, and list every place it fails or surprises, most serious first. Then set out the redesign options for where a deleted or replaced row lives, at least these three, with what each changes in the schema, in each verb, and in cost at the reference deployment: tombstones leave the path's unique key (a key over live rows only, and whether each of the five dialects can declare one: SQLite, Postgres, MariaDB, SQL Server, Oracle); tombstones are moved to a path under the trash view; the unique key gains a generation.

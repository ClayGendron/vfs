# The lexical build's chunk scan pays 35 s of its 61 s to a Bloom filter SQLite rebuilds on every one of 1,809 pages, and three bounded shapes that keep the SQL Server rule bring it to 3.3 s

Study 03 of the brief in [00-brief.md](00-brief.md), following up the figure [study 02](02-where-the-sixty-four-microseconds-go.md) attributed by subtraction: about 36 s of the 100,000-file rebuild's `build_lexical_epoch` in the keyset chunk scan of `_chunk_batches` in `src/vfs/storage/backends/database/lexical.py`. This study measures the scan by timer, explains the page statement on SQLite and on Postgres, and measures the bounded alternatives the brief names, each asserting the same epoch rows. Machine: Apple M1 Pro, 10 cores, 32 GiB, macOS Darwin 25.5.0; Python 3.13.11 with its bundled SQLite 3.50.4 (what aiosqlite runs); the statement-status counters read through ctypes against Homebrew libsqlite3 3.53.4; Postgres 17 in the `pgvector/pgvector:pg17` test container, tmpfs data directory; SQLAlchemy 2.0.52; vfs at commit b85e3df. Time box: about three hours; everything the brief asked for ran (eleven SQLite rebuilds, the Postgres build and three Postgres rebuilds), and what was left out is named in the last section.

## 1. The answer in one paragraph

EXECUTED and READ. The scan costs 35.06 s of the 60.62 s lexical build (57.8%) and 47.0% of the 74.57 s rebuild: 1,809 pages of 256 rows at a median of 19.08 ms a page, flat from the first page to the last, 377 KB of content a page. SQLite's plan for the page is the right one, chunks by primary key then one unique-index probe per row into `vfs_entries`, with no sort and no full scan, but it carries a `BLOOM FILTER ON vfs_entries` step: because the table has `ANALYZE` statistics and the planner's estimate of the outer loop (`id > ?` with no regard for the `LIMIT`) exceeds the entry table's row count, it builds a Bloom filter over every qualifying entry row, and it builds it again on every execution, so each page spends 916,385 virtual-machine steps, about 914,000 of them scanning 103,921 entry rows into a filter that all 256 probes then pass (`filter_miss=256, filter_hit=0`). The same page without the join is 1,544 steps and 0.28 ms. Three shapes that keep the docstring's SQL Server rule (no cursor open while the build writes) remove the cost with identical epoch rows: the entry filter as a correlated scalar subquery the planner does not flatten (scan 3.64 s, 1.95 ms a page, no new statements), the qualifying entry ids drained once into a Python set and the chunks paged alone (3.34 s), and the two-phase id-then-content fetch under the 1,000-element Oracle floor (2.09 s plus a 4.9 s id drain). Bigger pages only amortize the filter (12.6 s at 1,024, 3.7 s at 16,384), a temp table makes it worse (97 s), and the `IN (SELECT ...)` subquery is the worst shape of all (204 s). On Postgres the shipped page plans as a nested loop over the chunk primary key and a memoized unique-index probe with no filter step, 0.3 to 0.7 ms of execution and 3.8 ms a page through the driver, 7.9 s for the scan; the Python set matches it (7.4 s plus 0.9 s), the two-phase fetch is slower (13.6 s), and the scalar subquery is a regression there because Postgres JIT-compiles its SubPlan filter at 5 to 7 ms a page, so the shape that serves both engines is one whose entry filter neither planner sees in the page: the chunks paged alone with the qualifying ids drained once, or a dialect-conditional expression.

## 3. Why a page costs 19 ms: the plan, the counters, and the SQLite source

EXECUTED, `EXPLAIN QUERY PLAN` of the exact statement (compiled with literal binds by the harness; `explain_sqlite.txt`) on Python's SQLite 3.50.4: `SEARCH vfs_chunks USING INTEGER PRIMARY KEY (rowid>?)`, `BLOOM FILTER ON vfs_entries (entry_id=?)`, `SEARCH vfs_entries USING INDEX ix_vfs_entries_entry_id (entry_id=?)`. INFERRED from the table. The content bytes are not the cost (ids-only costs the same 12.6 ms); the join's lookups are not the cost (the unfiltered join costs 0.35 ms); the 914,000 extra steps a page are the Bloom filter's construction, about 8.8 steps for each of the 103,921 entry rows, and `filter_miss=256` with `filter_hit=0` says the filter admitted every probe and excluded nothing, because every chunk row's entry qualifies. The `LIMIT` series fits 12.7 ms fixed plus about 1.6 µs a row. The in-process walk is 23.4 s against the harness's 35.06 s: the remaining 6.3 ms a page is the aiosqlite thread hop, SQLAlchemy's `Row` construction and the text decode of 377 KB, which the harness also pays on the cheap shapes (1.95 ms a page for the scalar subquery against 0.43 in-process).

READ, `src/where.c` of the SQLite clone at 3d17c3f9e. `whereCheckIfBloomFilterIsUseful` sets `WHERE_BLOOMFILTER` on an inner loop when the table has `ANALYZE` statistics (`TF_HasStat1`, the comment's condition 4; the loop breaks without them), the loop is an equality lookup whose WHERE terms are expected to cull rows (`WHERE_SELFCULL | WHERE_COLUMN_EQ`), and the accumulated estimated searches from the outer loops exceed the table's estimated rows (`nSearch > pTab->nRowLogEst`). The outer loop here is `vfs_chunks` under `id > ?`, estimated as a fraction of 462,963 without regard to the `LIMIT`, and that exceeds the 93,921 estimated entry rows. `sqlite3ConstructBloomFilter` then emits the filter's construction behind an `OP_Once`, which runs once per execution of the statement, scanning the entry table and adding each row that passes the WHERE terms; the comment in the same file says the filter exists to skip B-tree seeks that would find nothing. The optimization entered the tree on 2021-12-04 ("First attempt to use Bloom filters to optimize star-schema queries") and shipped in 3.38.0.

EXECUTED, `check_stat1_sqlite.txt`. With the store's `sqlite_stat1` rows deleted, the plan changes to `SEARCH vfs_entries USING INDEX ix_vfs_entries_deleted_at`, `SEARCH vfs_chunks USING INDEX ix_vfs_chunks_entry_id (entry_id=? AND rowid>?)`, `USE TEMP B-TREE FOR ORDER BY`: entries outer, every qualifying entry's chunks collected and sorted for each page, 68.5 ms and 1,623,201 steps a page, worse than the filter. With `ANALYZE` run again the Bloom plan returns at 15.2 ms. The statistics vfs gathers since commit 2d263fb ("a SQLite store gathers its own planner statistics", `PRAGMA optimize` through `statistics_listing` in `dialects.py`) are what make the planner choose the filter, and without them the page is worse still, so the statistics are not the thing to remove.

EXECUTED, `probe_shapes_sqlite.txt`, eight spellings of the page at `last` = 231,481. Still the Bloom plan at about 13 ms: the shipped join; `CROSS JOIN` with chunks outer (the order was already right); `likelihood(term, 0.99)` and `likely(term)` on the three entry terms (the planner still counts them as culling); unary plus on the terms; the filter moved into the `ON` clause; and a correlated `EXISTS (SELECT 1 ...)`, which this SQLite flattens into the join and then filters (1,020,562 steps). The one spelling that escapes is the scalar subquery compared to a constant, `(SELECT 1 FROM vfs_entries WHERE entry_id = vfs_chunks.entry_id AND filter) = 1`: a correlated scalar subquery the planner leaves in place, one unique-index probe per chunk row, 6,409 steps and 0.43 ms a page, a full walk of 1,810 pages in 0.92 s.

INFERRED. The cause is a planner heuristic whose cost model has no `LIMIT` term and no notion that a statement will be executed 1,809 times with a filter that passes everything; on this corpus the filter's build is 97% of the page and its benefit is zero. It is also a function of the entry table's size, not the page's: each page scans every entry row, so the scan's total is pages times entries, which is chunks times entries over the page size, a product of two corpus dimensions. Section 6 multiplies it out.

EXECUTED, the memory the brief asked for. Two-phase: 462,963 `(id, entry_id)` tuples measure 70.2 MB by `sys.getsizeof` summed over list, tuples, ints and 26-character strings, and 72.0 MB current and peak by `tracemalloc` in a separate drain of the same statement (`measure_pairs_memory.txt`), while the process RSS grew by 960 MB in that drain and the peak RSS by 817 MB inside the harness: the difference is the mapped database file (`PRAGMA mmap_size` 8 GiB, `cache_size` 256 MiB in `dialects.py`), whose pages the join touches, not Python heap. Streaming-buffered: 462,963 `lex_docs` row dicts, 139.1 MB by `getsizeof`, written in 3.07 s after the cursor closed. Prefilter set: 100,000 entry ids, 10.4 MB by `getsizeof`, drained in 0.82 s.

INFERRED from the table, one line per shape. (a) Bigger pages amortize the filter's fixed 12.7 ms and nothing else: 1,024 rows cost 27 ms a page (the filter plus 1,024 rows' worth of Row construction), and even 16,384-row pages, which hold 24 MB of content each and lift the process's transient memory by that much, leave 3.7 s of scan because 30 executions still build the filter 30 times; the shape is the same cost paid fewer times. (b) The two-phase fetch is the only one that touches each table once for its purpose: the id stream (one statement, one filter build, 4.9 s of which about 4 s is 462,963 `Row` objects through the driver) and then content by primary-key membership, 2.1 s at the Oracle floor of 1,000 ids a probe, 463 probes at 4.2 ms, with 70 MB of ids held; the dialect's own budget of 32,668 makes 15 probes of 133 ms and 48 MB of content each, no faster in total, so the floor is the better shape on every engine. (c) The streaming cursor costs what the gram feed costs, 3.3 s, and holds 139 MB of `lex_docs` rows until the end, and it is the shape the docstring forbids on a driver without multiple active result sets. (d) The temp table is a trap on SQLite: the join to `tmp_covered` plans with a Bloom filter too, and a temp table without statistics makes the planner's estimates worse (53 ms a page), while the Python set does the same filtering client-side for 10 MB and 0.8 s and leaves the page a bare primary-key range at 1.8 ms; it dropped 0 rows here because every chunk's entry qualifies, and on a store with many non-indexable or deleted entries the pages would carry the dropped rows' content across the driver before being discarded. (e) The `IN (SELECT ...)` form is the worst shape, a full entry scan plus a sort per page; the scalar-subquery form is the smallest change (one expression in one statement, no new round trips, no client state, same statement count) and brings the scan to 3.64 s with the consumer unchanged.

INFERRED. The consumer's time (`yield`) is 19.8 to 20.0 s in the two baseline samples, the scalar subquery and the streaming run, and 23.0 to 23.8 s in the other eight; the difference is in `add_docs` (16.9 to 17.1 against 19.7 to 19.9 s) and the inserts, Rust and SQLite work the scan shape does not touch, so it is run-to-run variance of this machine (the fast runs were the first of their sequence or ran alone after an idle period, which points at thermal state or cache), not a property of the variants. The scan wall column is the figure to compare; `build_lexical_epoch` and `reindex` carry this noise.

## 6. The projection

The reference deployment runs Postgres, where the shipped scan costs about 14.5 CPU-minutes a day of a 73-minute lexical build and no alternative measured here improves it; the finding there is that the driver's per-row cost (15 µs for a 1.5 KB row, 3 ms a page) is the scan's floor and the builder and inserts are the build. On SQLite, the dev and test default and a store some deployments will run, the shipped shape's cost is quadratic in the corpus and the fix is one of three. Among the shapes that keep the docstring's rule without a second connection: the scalar subquery changes the fewest things (one expression in one statement) and brings the SQLite scan to 3.64 s, but is a 2.5× regression on Postgres unless it is spelled per dialect; the Python set holds one 26-character id per covered entry (10 MB here, about 1.1 GB at 11,000,000 entries) and is within noise of the shipped shape on Postgres while bringing SQLite to 3.34 s, so it is the one shape that serves both engines with one spelling, at the cost of shipping non-covered chunks' content across the driver on a store with many deleted or non-indexable entries; the two-phase fetch is the shape to reach for when a build must also skip chunk rows by id (it holds them all), and the slowest of the three on Postgres. The streaming cursor is the fastest and the one the constraint forbids. On SQLite the pure scan floor is the chunks-alone walk, 0.61 s in-process for 1,810 pages, so the scalar subquery's 3.64 s and the set's 3.34 s are about 1.5 ms a page of driver and `Row` cost above the engine, the same cost the gram feed pays.

## What this study does not settle

The scalar-subquery shape was measured on SQLite 3.50.4 and 3.53.4 and on Postgres 17; whether SQL Server, Oracle and MariaDB plan it as one index probe per row, and whether any of them flattens it into a join with its own pushdown, is the experiment: the conformance suite's engine legs with `EXPLAIN` of the page on each, which the `db_test` skill brings up.

Whether SQLite's planner takes the `LIMIT` into `nSearch` in any later release, which would make the shipped join cheap again without a change here, is a read of `where.c` on each release and a rerun of `explain_sqlite.py` on the pinned Python's SQLite; this study read 3.54.0 and measured 3.50.4 and 3.53.4.

The fast consumer runs (`add_docs` 16.9 to 17.1 s against 19.7 to 19.9 s) are attributed to run order; the experiment is the baseline and the scalar subquery run three times each, interleaved with an idle minute between runs and without, reporting `add_docs` per run.

The Postgres build's own `reindex()` took 759 s where the prior study's took 147 s and this study's timed rebuilds took 80 s on analysed tables; the first rebuild of a freshly written schema runs before autovacuum has analysed it, and the experiment is `EXPLAIN` of the page statement and of the gram scan on a fresh unanalysed schema beside the harness's page timers, since a plan without statistics may be the SQLite no-statistics shape (entries outer, sort per page) on Postgres too.

The scalar subquery and the streaming cursor were not run on Postgres in the harness (the former's regression was measured by `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` and five driver executions a page, the latter is the shape the constraint forbids); the experiment is `harness.py --engine postgres --variant scalar_subquery` and `--variant stream_buffered`, each about 80 s plus the WAL and vacuum housekeeping the tmpfs needs between runs.

A store where many chunks belong to non-covered entries (deleted, not indexable) was not built; on it the Python-set shape ships dropped rows' content across the driver and the scalar subquery probes and discards them in the engine, and the experiment is the harness on a copy with a third of the entries tombstoned, counting dropped rows and bytes.

The reference deployment's chunk count (10 a file) is twice this store's ratio, so every per-visit figure here understates the scan's share of the build by about that factor; the experiment is the same harness on a store built from bodies at the reference's median 4 KB and mean 0.5 MB, counting chunks per file.

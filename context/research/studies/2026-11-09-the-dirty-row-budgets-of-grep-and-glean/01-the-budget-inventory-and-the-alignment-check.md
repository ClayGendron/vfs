## The answer in one paragraph

INFERRED from the table below: the two verbs nominate the scan tier through one shared helper in one order, so what they drop is the same kind of thing, but they bound it in different shapes. Grep bounds the scan tier by count (what is left of a 25,000-candidate pool the index side draws from first), by bytes (32 MiB verify batches), and by wall (10 s, consulted between ladder stages, between arm chunks, between content batches and inside the matcher). Glean bounds its overlay by count alone (a fixed 500, independent of the index side), fetches every admitted body in one unbatched call, and runs its tokenize-and-score hop with no deadline inside it. The 25,000 was measured on a sweep (spec 103, 2026-08-17); the 500 was declared with the lexical leg (spec 132, 2026-08-27) and its own spec calls it "not a measured optimum", while the memo that shaped the leg had recommended "the same candidate budget and deadline" as grep's scan tier. The records a caller gets are the same kind, severity and sentence shape; glean's carries structured data and grep's none. At the reference deployment's write rate every cadence in the table exceeds 500 dirty entries within minutes of an index pass, so glean's ranking at that scale is always short of the dirty set and always carries the warning, while grep's scan tier holds the whole dirty set at 15-minute and hourly cadence and loses it only at rebuild-only cadence or under a pattern whose index side alone fills the pool.

## 2. The alignment check

### 2a. Shape of the bound

READ: grep's scan tier is bounded in three shapes. Count: `limit = CANDIDATE_BUDGET - len(candidates)` (`grep.py:291, 317`), so the scan tier gets what the index side left of one 25,000 pool, and gets nothing when the index side filled it. Bytes: bodies are fetched, matched and released in `CONTENT_BYTE_BUDGET` batches by exact `size_bytes` (`grep.py:356-362`). Wall: the deadline is consulted after the ladder (`grep.py:286`), between arm chunks inside the nomination (`scope.py:293`), in the gate hop (`grep.py:325-329`), before every content batch (`grep.py:357`), and inside the matcher, whose budget is cut at worker start (`offload.py:185-187, 203-205`) so a batch that runs long stops mid-batch with the record (`grep.py:388-393`).

READ: glean's overlay is bounded in one shape. Count: `limit=OVERLAY_BUDGET` (`glean.py:419`), a fixed 500 whatever the index side did. Bytes: `_overlay` fetches every admitted body in one `content_for_entries` call (`glean.py:986`), chunked only by `membership_budget` for the statement's `IN` list (`reads.py:710-712`), so all admitted bodies are resident at once with no byte budget. Wall: the deadline is consulted before the nomination (`glean.py:408`), between arm chunks inside it (`scope.py:293`) and in the judge hop (`glean.py:425-427`); the tokenize-and-score hop at `glean.py:988` is `call_offloaded(executor, partial(_overlay_blocks, docs, terms, stats))` with no deadline argument, and `_overlay_blocks` (`glean.py:1016-1044`) reads no clock, so once the bodies are in hand the hop runs to completion however long it takes.

INFERRED: the real difference between the verbs is that glean scores and ranks where grep matches, so a partial overlay changes the order of the answer (a dirty file that would have ranked first is simply absent, and the min-max scale at `glean.py:1072-1076` is taken over what was scored) while a partial scan tier in grep loses matches outright. That difference argues for a coherent, deterministic cut, which both verbs have through path order; it does not argue for the cut being 50 times smaller, and it does not argue against byte batching or an in-hop deadline, which are about residency and latency, not ranking. The glean memo that shaped the leg asked for the same budget and deadline as grep's scan tier (READ, memo line 136); spec 132 declared 500 and said it was unmeasured (READ, line 94). The three shape divergences are therefore drift, not verb differences: the count (500 against a 25,000 pool), the absent byte batching, and the absent in-hop deadline. The grep side moved its own deadline inside the verify loop after the profile memo caught a 10 s budget running 102 s (READ [spec 103](../../../specifications/archive/103-grep-pipeline-rust-core/spec.md) line 31; [the profile memo](../../2026-08-16-grep-read-path-profile.md) line 30); the overlay hop was written after that fix and did not take it.

INFERRED: one shape difference is justified by structure. Grep's scan `limit` is the remainder of a pool the index side draws from first, because grep's index side produces candidate entries that compete for the same verify work; glean's index side produces a ranking bounded by `FUSION_K` chunks and resolves them to entries separately (`glean.py:352-364`), so there is no shared entry pool to subtract from. A fixed overlay count is the natural shape for glean. The consequence to record is that grep can skip the overlay entirely on a busy pattern and says so by name, while glean never skips it for budget reasons; glean skips it only past the deadline (`glean.py:408`), and then the only record is the wall record, which does not name the overlay.

### 2b. What the records say

READ: both verbs emit `ErrorKind.truncated` at `Severity.warning` with `detail = f"at the {reason}; {guidance}"` (`grep.py:394-396`; `glean.py:459-467`), both ride the `Result.errors` list without failing the call (`test_grep.py:645-646`, `test_glean.py:446-449` assert `success is True`), and both use the same wall cause string. The reason names differ: grep says `candidate budget` or `candidate budget, with the unindexed overlay not consulted`; glean says `overlay budget`. The guidance differs by the word `pattern` against `query`. Grep's records carry `data=None` always; glean's overlay record carries `{"scanned": 500, "budget": 500}` and its window records `{"window", "found"}`, while its wall record carries `None` (`glean.py:1181`).

INFERRED: the records say the same kind of thing (a runtime budget cut the answer; narrow or scope to shrink the work) in the same severity and sentence shape, which is aligned. The payload asymmetry is minor drift: a caller of grep cannot read how far over the pool the call was, and a caller of glean reads `scanned` and `budget` but neither verb reports how many dirty entries were left out, because the nomination statement is `LIMIT limit + 1` and never counts the rest. Neither record names a dropped path. The guidance is actionable for both, since `globs`, `ext` and `scope` are pushed into the nomination statement (`scope.py:273-289`) and so change which dirty rows are nominated, not only which are returned. The one caller-visible difference in meaning is that grep's "overlay not consulted" variant tells an agent plainly that a freshly written file may be missing; glean's "overlay budget" variant tells it that 500 were scored and does not say that the ranking may be missing the file it just wrote.

### 2c. Path order and what is dropped

READ: `entries_for_scan` orders every nomination statement `ORDER BY entry.c.path` with `LIMIT limit + 1` (`scope.py:295, 306`), merges by path, prunes to the lowest `limit + 1` paths (`scope.py:299-300`), and returns the lowest `limit` (`scope.py:308-311`). Both verbs pass the same gates, wanted exts, scope roots and scope facts (`grep.py:307-322`; `glean.py:409-424`); only `limit` and `everything` differ.

INFERRED: under the same scope, the two verbs drop from the same end of the same order, the highest-sorting dirty paths, so when both overflow the set glean drops is a superset of the set grep drops, and a dirty file that grep drops is one glean drops too. They do not drop the same files, because the cut points differ: glean's is 500, grep's is 25,000 minus the index candidates, which can be anywhere from 25,000 down to zero. Path order is collation order over the whole stored path, so the files dropped are those under the directories whose names sort last, systematically and on every call, with no relation to when they were written. At the reference deployment's shape (about 1,000,000 directories, dirty rows a 0.5% sample of the tree spread by the write pattern), a freshly written file survives the cut if its directory sorts early enough among the dirty set's directories, and never survives if its top-level directory sorts after the cut, so an agent working under a late-sorting root (`/wiki`, `/src/z…`) is the one the overlay fails for, every time, until the next index pass. Recency order (the row's `version` or write stamp) or a stable random order would serve the "file I just wrote" case differently; which is right is not this study's call.

INFERRED from the statement shape: `ORDER BY path LIMIT 501` over the `NOT encoded` partition is a sort of every dirty row's key regardless of the cut, so the nomination's cost grows with the dirty set, not with the budget, on any engine that does not walk the path index for this predicate; the SQLite profile's `unlikely()` hint (`dialects.py:370-378`, `scope.py:269`) exists to keep the seek on the `(encoded, kind)` index and then sort, so at 10,000 dirty rows glean reads 10,000 index entries to return 501. This is a cost-shape claim that study 02 measures; it is not measured here.

### 2d. Measured against inherited

EXECUTED and READ: measured numbers are `CANDIDATE_BUDGET = 25_000` (the spec 103 §5 sweep on the linux tree, ~75 µs per candidate, resolved 2026-08-17 and re-confirmed by the scoped bench docstring "All budgets stand as derived"), the `_PATTERN_ARM_CEILING = 200` behind `arm_budget` ("measured OR-fan sweet spot", `dialects.py:720-722`), `OFFLOAD_WORKERS` (measured plateau, `offload.py:104-109`), the `(encoded, kind)` index that serves both verbs' skip (spec 097's EXPLAIN), and the `in_list_budget` floors, which are engine facts (Oracle's 1,000, SQL Server's 2,100 binds) rather than vfs measurements. The same study measured grep's scan tax (10 patterns: 1.0 s clean and 4.0 s at 500 dirty on 10k; 13.4 s clean and 24–27 s at 500–2,500 dirty on 50k) and saw no match lost (lines 74-110).

### 2e. The vector leg

READ: a content write stamps the row `"chunked": False, "encoded": encoded_at_creation(kind)` (`writes.py:1207-1208`), and `encoded_at_creation` is true for a directory only (`src/vfs/models/entry.py:46-54`), so a written content row is `NOT encoded` and `NOT chunked`. The old chunk rows stay until the chunk step re-splits them (READ [the glean memo](../../2026-08-26-glean-in-the-engine.md) line 133; `indexing.py:327` selects the `~entry.c.chunked` rows during `reindex` and restamps `"chunked": True` at `indexing.py:350, 352`). The vector leg's statement carries `chunks.c.embedding.isnot(None)` and `entry.c.chunked` (`glean.py:751-752`), so a dirty entry's old chunks are excluded by the `chunked` predicate and its new chunks do not exist yet. The embed step fills `embedding IS NULL` rows of `entry.c.chunked` entries (`embed.py:208-226`) and runs only inside `reindex`, after the index phases, when an embedder is configured (`backend.py:1182-1215, 1544-1557`). The overlay scores lexically only: `_overlay` returns `_Hit(score, [(-1, score)])` per entry (`glean.py:991`), the result is merged into the lexical `hits` (`glean.py:434`) and fused as the `"lexical"` leg (`glean.py:1072-1081`); `vector_hits` come from `_vector_leg` alone (`glean.py:379`). Confirmed: a dirty entry has no vector until the embed step, and nothing in the overlay covers the vector leg.

INFERRED: this is justified by cost, since covering the vector leg at query time would mean embedding every dirty body through the provider per call, and the fusion renormalises over the legs present (`glean.py:1089`, docstring at `glean.py:1063-1066`) so a lexical-only dirty hit is not penalised for having no vector leg. It is still a disclosed asymmetry between the two sides of the partition: an indexed entry is ranked by both legs and a dirty entry by one, and no record says so.

## 3. INFERRED: what each budget means at the reference deployment

INFERRED from [AGENTS.md](../../../../AGENTS.md) "The reference deployment" and the brief: the dirty set is write rate times time since the last index pass, so at 50,000 organic writes a working day (about 104 a minute over eight hours; 208 at the 100,000 ceiling) it is about 1,250 to 2,500 at a 15-minute refresh, 5,000 to 10,000 at an hourly refresh, and tens of thousands by afternoon with rebuild-only cadence (50,000 by the end of the day at the centre, 100,000 at the ceiling). The nightly batch of 1,000,000 is indexed by the rebuild and does not add to the daytime dirty set. Corpus size does not set these figures; a 100,000-file store with the same write rate has the same dirty set.

INFERRED: `OVERLAY_BUDGET = 500` is exceeded under every cadence in the table. At 104 writes a minute the dirty set passes 500 about five minutes after any pass (2.4 minutes at the ceiling), so at a 15-minute refresh glean's lexical leg scores 20 to 40% of the dirty set for most of each interval, at an hourly refresh 5 to 10%, and at rebuild-only cadence 1 to 5%, always the lowest-sorting paths. Every glean call in working hours carries the `overlay budget` warning, which makes the record a constant rather than a signal. This is drift from the memo's recommendation and from grep's shape, not a justified difference, and the only argument for keeping it at 500 would be a measured cost curve that shows 2,500 is unaffordable, which no memo holds; study 02's job.

INFERRED: `CANDIDATE_BUDGET = 25_000` as the scan cut holds the whole dirty set at 15-minute and hourly cadence (2,500 and 10,000 are inside the pool) provided the index side left the room, and loses it at rebuild-only cadence (50,000 dirty exceeds the pool even with an empty index side), with the `candidate budget` record. Costed by the spec 103 constant of ~75 µs per candidate, the scan tier adds about 0.19 s at 2,500 dirty, 0.75 s at 10,000 and 1.9 s at the 25,000 cut per grep call, under the 10 s wall; the baseline study's measured 2 to 4× tax at 500 to 2,500 dirty on 10k and 50k stores is the empirical anchor. The pool's shape matters more at 10,000,000 files than at 50,000: the baseline saw a three-letter word fill 25,000 index candidates on a 50k store and skip the overlay by design (READ, line 108), and at the reference scale any common word does, so on a common pattern grep too answers without the dirty set and says so by name. Justified as a latency bound, with the shared-pool consequence to be weighed by the memo.

INFERRED: glean's unbatched body fetch at 500 nominees holds about 2 MB at the 4 KB median and up to 100 MB if every nominee is at the 200 KB tail, three times grep's 32 MiB residency law in the tail case; at any larger budget the residency grows linearly, so lifting the 500 without batching would trade one drift for another. Drift, and the fix is grep's existing law (`byte_chunked` with `CONTENT_BYTE_BUDGET`), which the design-options study already named as hygiene (READ [04-design-options](../2026-10-31-incremental-index-refresh/04-design-options.md) line 190, "D1 as hygiene, independent of refresh").

INFERRED: the absent deadline inside the overlay's tokenize-and-score hop means a 10 s `glean_wall_seconds` is not a bound on that hop; at 500 bodies the memo's 1.8 ms per entry in pure Python (now Rust tokenisation) keeps it under a second, so the gap is latent at 500 and becomes live at any budget that approaches the wall. Drift; grep's `VerifyOffload` is the pattern.

INFERRED: `WALL_TIME_BUDGET = 10.0 s` is the same number on both verbs with the same knob shape and the same record, so it is aligned; at ~75 µs per candidate it would bind only past about 130,000 candidates, so the count caps bind first on both verbs and the wall is the backstop. INFERRED: path order is shared by both verbs and therefore aligned, but at the reference scale it is the wrong kind of determinism for an agent audience, because the file an agent just wrote is dropped or kept by the name of its directory and not by anything the agent can see or change. INFERRED: the divergences, named. ## 4. What this study does not settle

The cost per dirty entry of each verb's scan tier at 500, 2,000, 5,000, 10,000 and 25,000 dirty on SQLite and Postgres, split for glean into nominee fetch, body fetch and tokenize-and-score, and the N at which each verb crosses the 10 s wall: study 02 in this folder, with `OVERLAY_BUDGET` monkeypatched to 25,000 in its harness.

Whether the nomination statement's cost is linear in the dirty set regardless of the cut (section 2c's claim): an `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` on Postgres and `EXPLAIN QUERY PLAN` on SQLite of `entries_for_scan`'s statement at 10,000 dirty rows with `LIMIT 501` and `LIMIT 25,001`, counting rows visited.

Whether a paged overlay that batches bodies by `CONTENT_BYTE_BUDGET` and carries the deadline into the score hop reaches 2,500 dirty entries inside one second at the 4 KB median: an experiment on study 02's corpus after its curve is known, scoring with the current `_overlay_blocks` under a monkeypatched budget and a harness-side batcher.

Whether a recency or stable random nomination order costs more than path order at the reference shape: an experiment comparing `ORDER BY path`, `ORDER BY version DESC` and a hashed order over the `(encoded, kind)` partition at 10,000 and 50,000 dirty rows on Postgres, counting rows visited and sort work.

Whether a truncation record can afford to carry the dirty total: an experiment costing `COUNT(*)` over the `NOT encoded AND kind IN (…)` partition at 10,000,000 rows on Postgres against the per-call multiplier of about 150,000 calls a day.

How often a common pattern's index side alone fills the 25,000 pool at 1,000,000 files, so the overlay is never consulted: an experiment on a 1,000,000-file store with the baseline study's ten patterns, counting the "overlay not consulted" records.

## One line

Both verbs drop the same end of the same path order, but grep bounds its scan tier by a measured count, bytes and a wall while glean bounds its overlay by one declared count that every reference-scale cadence exceeds within minutes; the count, the missing byte batches and the missing in-hop deadline are drift, the fixed-against-remainder shape and the lexical-only overlay are justified.

## The questions

Researcher 01, proofs. What can be mathematically proven about a Python-plus-Rust library, and with what. Cover deductive and bounded verifiers for Rust (Kani, Verus, Creusot, Prusti, Aeneas, Flux, and AWS's effort to verify the Rust standard library), for Python (CrossHair, Nagini, deal, and anything newer), and design-level model checkers and specification languages (TLA+ and Apalache, P, Alloy, Quint, Lean as used for specifications). For each, say what class of property it proves (absence of panics and overflow, functional correctness against a specification, a protocol invariant), what it must trust (the compiler, the interpreter, the SQL engine, the specification itself), and whether it can reach code that crosses pyo3 or issues SQL. Study the case studies that matter most: seL4 and CompCert (what end-to-end proof costs), AWS's lightweight formal methods on S3's ShardStore, and Cedar, the authorization language verified in Lean with differential testing against its Rust implementation (clone at `/Users/claygendron/Git/Repos/cedar`; also study `cedar-spec` on GitHub, cloning it shallow if its source is the best evidence). Cedar matters most because vfs's central claim is an authorization claim. Where cheap, install Kani or CrossHair in scratch and run it on a ten-line example to report what using it is like. Your file: `context/research/studies/2026-11-12-how-far-vfs-can-prove-it-does-what-it-says/01-what-can-be-proven-and-with-what.md`.

Researcher 02, evidence short of proof. How the most trusted software earns its trust without a full proof. Study SQLite's testing (the clone at `/Users/claygendron/Git/Repos/sqlite` and https://sqlite.org/testing.html: TH3, 100% MC/DC branch coverage, the fuzzers, the ratio of test code to code, the assertions, the anomaly tests), deterministic simulation testing (FoundationDB, TigerBeetle's VOPR, Turso's simulator in the clone at `/Users/claygendron/Git/Repos/turso`, Antithesis as a service), Jepsen and Elle for databases, differential testing (Cedar's differential tests, Csmith, SQLancer for SQL engines, sqlsmith), mutation testing (mutmut, cosmic-ray and mutatest for Python, cargo-mutants for Rust: what a mutation score says that line coverage does not), fuzzing (cargo-fuzz and libFuzzer, Atheris for Python, OSS-Fuzz and its admission criteria), property-based and stateful testing (Hypothesis and its stateful machines, proptest), and coverage measures stronger than line coverage (branch, MC/DC, and what Python's coverage.py and Rust's `cargo llvm-cov` can and cannot measure). For each, say what kind of bug it finds that the others miss, and what a passing result is worth as a claim to a stranger. Where cheap, run mutmut or cargo-mutants on a few-function toy in scratch and report what the output looks like. Your file: `context/research/studies/2026-11-12-how-far-vfs-can-prove-it-does-what-it-says/02-evidence-short-of-proof.md`.

Researcher 03, seals and attestations. What seal, badge, certification or attestation a library can carry that tells a stranger they can trust it, and what each one actually attests. Cover the OpenSSF Best Practices Badge at its passing, silver and gold levels (read the criteria; say which ones a solo-maintainer project can and cannot meet and why), OpenSSF Scorecard (its checks and what a score means), SLSA build levels and provenance, Sigstore signing, PyPI Trusted Publishing and PEP 740 digital attestations, GitHub artifact attestations, reproducible builds, software bills of materials (CycloneDX, SPDX), cargo-vet and cargo-crev, OSS-Fuzz membership, independent security audits (OSTIF, Trail of Bits, NCC Group, Cure53: what they cost and what their reports promise), and the regulatory and enterprise frames a buyer will ask about (the EU Cyber Resilience Act's obligations for open-source stewards and manufacturers, SOC 2 and ISO 27001 and why they attach to an organisation and a service rather than a library, FIPS 140-3 and when it applies). Separate the seals that attest how software was built and delivered (provenance, signing) from those that attest how a project is run (practices) from those that attest what the code does (none of the badges): say plainly which kind each is. Name well-known Python and Rust projects that carry each seal and what they display. Read the vfs `.github/workflows/` and `pyproject.toml` read-only to say what vfs's publish pipeline already does and which seals are one workflow change away. Your file: `context/research/studies/2026-11-12-how-far-vfs-can-prove-it-does-what-it-says/03-seals-and-what-each-attests.md`.

Researcher 04, vfs's own claims and what pins them today. Read the vfs tree read-only and list the claims vfs makes about itself, in its `README.md`, `AGENTS.md`, `documentation/`, the module docstrings under `src/vfs/`, and the active specifications under `context/specifications/active/`: the claims a stranger would rely on (permissions are enforced before anything is dispatched; no statement grows with batch size; unknown dialects are served the core verbs; the Rust engine and the oracles agree; results are classified rather than raised; and whatever else you find). For each claim, find what pins it today (the tests by path, the oracles and parity tests, the lint in `tests/lint/`, the coverage gate in `scripts/ci.sh`, the real-engine legs in `.github/workflows/`), and grade the pin: proven for every input, tested on chosen examples, tested by generated inputs, checked by a lint, or stated and unpinned. Then, for the claims that matter most to a stranger, say which technique would raise each pin a grade (a property-based or stateful test, a model in a specification language, a bounded proof of a Rust kernel, a differential test against an engine, a mutation run) and what that would take, naming the files that would change. Run the suite's cheap pieces if they help you grade (`uv run pytest -m "not slow" -q`, `uv run pytest --collect-only -q | tail`), and count, by grep, how many tests use Hypothesis or any generated input today.
